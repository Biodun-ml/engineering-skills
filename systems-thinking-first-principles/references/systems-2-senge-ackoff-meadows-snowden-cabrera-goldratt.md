# systems-2 — cross-cutting principles

1. **A system is a whole that can't be split into independent parts; its defining properties emerge from interactions, not parts.** Improving parts separately can make the whole worse; judge every change by its effect on the whole. (Ackoff, both talks)
2. **Fit beats local best.** The car built from the best engine, best transmission, etc. doesn't run. Pick components (suppliers, libraries, pipeline stages) for how they fit, not individual benchmarks. (Ackoff)
3. **Design the whole first, then the parts (architect's principle).** Change a part only if the whole improves. (Ackoff)
4. **Dissolve before you solve.** Four treatments: absolve, resolve (good enough), solve (optimal), dissolve (redesign so the problem can't occur — matchbook striker on the back). (Ackoff, speech)
5. **The frame decides the solution space.** Elevator waits were a boredom problem, not a capacity problem; mirrors cost $500. Problem labels (marketing, technical) describe the observer, and problems are often best solved somewhere other than where they're noticed. (Ackoff, speech)
6. **Aim at what you want, not at removing what you don't want;** do idealised design first, then work back under constraints. Continuous improvement is not creativity; leaps come from discontinuity. (Ackoff, Beyond Continual Improvement)
7. **Effectiveness over efficiency.** Doing the wrong thing right makes you wronger; doing the right thing wrong produces correctable errors. A system's real purpose is shown by what it's paid for and how it behaves. (Ackoff, both)
8. **Think in closed loops: when you hear "A causes B", ask how B feeds back to A.** Every decision sits inside a loop: state -> compare with goal -> action -> new state. (Meadows)
9. **Stocks are what people look at; flows are the decisions.** Model the level (backlog, users, cash) and the valves that fill and drain it. (Meadows)
10. **Structure produces behaviour.** Reinforcing loop -> exponential growth/decline; balancing loop -> goal-seeking; both -> S-curve; add delays -> overshoot/oscillation. Exponential growth: find the reinforcing loop. Stuck despite pushing: find the balancing loop and its hidden goal. (Meadows)
11. **Expect nonlinearity and delays; set the time horizon first.** Returns saturate; delays that matter depend on horizon. Ignoring delays gets the dynamics wrong. Loop dominance shifts (oil: more money finds more oil until depletion dominates). (Meadows)
12. **Causal, not correlational.** Every arrow should name a mechanism; correlated variables often share a hidden third driver. (Meadows)
13. **Match method to domain (Cynefin).** Ordered (high constraint): use best/good practice and automation. Complex (entangled): parallel safe-to-fail probes. Chaotic (no constraint): act to stabilise. Don't call everything complex; order is valuable within its boundary. (Snowden)
14. **In complex systems you manage only boundaries, catalysts and energy allocation.** Set flexible boundaries, introduce probes, amplify beneficial attractors and dampen harmful ones; attractors can be catalysed but not created. Change modulators, not "drivers". (Snowden)
15. **Outcomes live in interactions, not individuals.** Change linkages and roles, not people; use cognitively diverse trios; innovation needs starvation, pressure and perspective shift. Watch weak signals through many raw micro-stories before thresholds flip. (Snowden)
16. **Measurement serves learning when the doers own it.** Toyota measures a lot but doesn't report up for control; top-down incentives and ranking breed fear and unknowable losses. No feedback at all also kills learning. (Senge via Deming)
17. **"The system" is a pattern of interdependence we re-enact daily; culture is the assumptions we can't see.** Reorgs and programmes touch the surface; lasting change reaches assumptions. Include the people most affected; go upstream to where the pattern forms. (Senge)
18. **Thinking is structuring information: Distinctions, Systems (part/whole), Relationships, Perspectives.** Use DSRP as a checklist; don't let over-specified instructions (or AI) take the thinking off your own desk. (Cabrera)
19. **Focus: find the one constraint that limits the whole and put effort there.** Effort on non-constraints doesn't raise system output. (Goldratt, clip is intro only)

## Peter Senge — The Fifth Discipline (talk to educators) (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Fln7GnBNWmo)
**"The system" is not a thing you can point at; it is a pattern of interdependence that people re-enact every day through their assumptions and actions — so lasting change has to reach the assumptions, not just the org chart.**

- Mental models / first principles
  - There is no physical "system" to show a child. What exists are patterns of interdependence created hour by hour by how people think and act. Corollary: "the system" is never only "them"; the people complaining about it are part of the pattern that sustains it. The most systemic change is also the most personal.
  - Culture = the assumptions we cannot see (Ed Schein's definition). Like fish and water, the deepest drivers of behaviour are invisible to insiders. Surface changes (reorgs, new programs, speeches, hiring/firing) leave those assumptions intact, so the old pattern reasserts itself.
  - The organising metaphor of the Industrial Age is the machine: a designed purpose, uniform outputs, efficiency, and control exercised from outside by an operator. School (fixed start/end ages, batch progression, "slow" = defective) and work (measure, incentivise, rank) are the same institution built on that metaphor (Deming's point).
  - Two kinds of control: biological self-regulation (homeostasis — the system holds its own essentials stable) versus industrial control (someone outside pushes on the parts). Healthy systems rely on the first.
  - Deming's two cornerstones: people are born with intrinsic motivation and joy in learning; the prevailing management system (grades, ranking, MBO, incentive pay) destroys it by replacing learning with performing for someone else's approval.
- Mechanisms / examples
  - Toyota measures heavily but has no standardised cost-control reporting up the hierarchy; measures are used by the people doing the work to learn, not by managers to punish. Senge (via accountant Tom Johnson, who studied Toyota for five years) argues this is why competitors copying the production system's tools never reached its quality and cost.
  - Visa International (as of the late 1990s; note: figures are dated): roughly 3,000 staff, governed by a member constitution, deliberately designed so power cannot concentrate — founder Dee Hock believed concentration kills innovation and motivation.
  - Xerox remanufacturable copier: part count cut from ~2,000 to ~200, one mechanical adjustment, 97% recyclable ("zero to landfill" target); remanufacturing much cheaper than building new. Interface (carpets) aimed to lease, not sell, carpets. Lesson: redefining what the customer actually wants (the service, not the object) changes the whole system design.
  - When the assembly line underperforms the reflex is to speed it up; the same reflex shows up as longer hours. Speed of reasoning gets mistaken for understanding (Niels Bohr anecdote: slow, but when he understood, he really understood).
  - Organisations that really learn show a rhythm between teams (people who need each other to get something done) and communities (the wider web that includes the people most affected). Teaching autonomous professionals to work as a team was the hardest step in the school example ("when I close the door I'm God in my universe" is the industrial-control mindset).
- Decisions & trade-offs
  - Measurement is not the enemy; who uses the measure and for what is the decision. Measures owned by the doers for self-assessment accelerate learning; measures reported upward to drive incentives produce fear and gaming. Having no feedback at all also kills learning.
  - Reorganising is what leaders reach for because it is one of the few levers they directly control — but it rarely touches the assumptions generating behaviour.
  - Nature does not quantify; numbers are a human artifact. What's most measurable is not what's most real (persistence, inspiration, trust).
- Rules of thumb
  - Before blaming "the system", ask which of your own assumptions and routines re-create the pattern.
  - Put the people most affected in the room (his point: why are the kids never present where adults plan their future?). For a product: users; for a pipeline: downstream consumers.
  - Go upstream: problems that show up downstream (in companies) are often formed in upstream processes (education). Fix the source, not the symptom.
  - Expect the traditional system to fight back harder as alternatives succeed; resistance is a sign the pattern is being touched.
- Failure modes
  - Turning a philosophy into a toolkit: "quality management" became "measure, incentivise, drive standards" — the opposite of Deming's intent. Copying visible practices without the underlying assumptions fails.
  - Ranking and incentive pay create losses that are "unknown and unknowable" — they don't show up in any report.
  - Concentrated control kills local learning and initiative.
- Relevance to Sam
  - Pipeline/data-quality metrics: let the person or job producing the data see its own quality metrics immediately (self-assessment loop) rather than only a top-down dashboard used to assign blame; incidents get fixed faster when the feedback loop is local and blameless.
  - For Trameter and side businesses (Riselist, Tereka): ask what the customer actually wants (the trip experience, financial calm), not the artifact (a booking button, a spreadsheet) — Interface/Xerox-style reframing can collapse complexity (2,000 parts to 200).
  - Creator agency: incentive schemes that rank creators by a single metric invite gaming; design metrics creators use to improve themselves.
  - When a system keeps snapping back after a fix (a recurring dirty-data pattern, a recurring booking failure), the cause is likely an unexamined assumption upstream, not the last component touched.

## Russell Ackoff — Systems Thinking Speech (on education) (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EbLh7rZ3rhU)
**A system is a whole that cannot be divided into independent parts, so optimising parts separately does not optimise — and often damages — the whole; the best move on a problem is often to dissolve it by redesigning the system, not to solve it.**

- Mental models / first principles
  - Definition of a system (three conditions): (1) two or more parts, each able to affect the whole's behaviour; (2) no part has an independent effect — how a part affects the whole depends on what other parts do; (3) any grouping of parts into subgroups has the same two properties. Summary: a whole that cannot be split into independent parts.
  - Consequence: a system's defining property (a car moves you; you are alive) is a property of the whole that no part has. It emerges from interactions, not from the sum of the parts. Take the system apart and both the whole and the parts lose their essential properties (an engine on the floor cannot even move itself).
  - Best-parts fallacy: take the best engine (Rolls-Royce), best transmission (Mercedes), etc. from 457 car models and you do not get the best car — you don't get a car at all, because the parts don't fit. Performance comes from fit and interaction. When a system runs as well as possible, none of its parts may be running as well as possible.
  - Content of the mind hierarchy: data (symbols describing properties) -> information (processed data; answers who/what/where/when/how many) -> knowledge (how-to; produces skill) -> understanding (why; explanations) -> wisdom (judging the ends). The first four improve efficiency; only wisdom addresses effectiveness. Drucker: doing things right vs doing the right thing. Doing the wrong thing right makes you "wronger"; doing the right thing wrong produces detectable mistakes you can learn from.
  - A system's real purpose is revealed by its behaviour and its incentives, not its declarations. "Health care" paid for treating sickness is a sickness-and-disability care system; it will tend to sustain sickness, even unconsciously.
- Mechanisms / examples
  - Elevator story: tenants complain of ~2-minute waits (standard ~20s). Engineers frame it as "elevators too slow/too few", study three options (add shafts, faster cars, computer dispatch) — all uneconomic. A junior personnel-psychology graduate reframes: the complaint is boredom, not time. Mirrors in the lobby, ~$500, problem dissolved. Lesson: the framing defines the solution space; the person outside the discipline found it, and only after the group stopped shooting down ideas.
  - Matchbook story: warnings printed on books didn't stop burns or lawsuits; moving the striker to the back of the cover removed the hazard at zero cost. Design dissolved it.
  - Mantua literacy story: kids weren't failing to read for lack of ability but motivation (oral culture, books socially dangerous). Showing Charlie Chaplin silent films with subtitles all day made every child learn to read in a semester. Fix the motive, not the instruction.
  - Elderly woman's death on tenement stairs: doctor says more doctors, economist says more welfare, architect says elevators, social worker says family estrangement. Discipline labels describe the observer, not the problem.
  - Case Institute summer teams given real municipal problems learned calculus on their own when they needed it; 95% passed two years of maths by exam.
- Four ways to treat a problem
  - Absolution: ignore it, hope it goes away.
  - Resolution: use past experience to find something good enough (satisficing; clinical, qualitative).
  - Solution: find the optimum via research/experiment (quantitative). No solution stays solved, and every solution creates new problems.
  - Dissolution: redesign the system so the problem can no longer occur. Superior, and done by design rather than research.
- Problems vs exercises vs questions vs messes
  - An exercise is a problem with the formulating information removed (case studies are exercises). Practising on exercises is boxing with one hand tied. Questions stripped of context have no single answer (2+3 depends on units/base).
  - Problems are abstractions pulled out of reality; reality is a system of interacting problems — a "mess". Messes are tackled by design: start with the whole, then fit parts to it.
- Decisions & trade-offs / rules of thumb
  - Architect's principle: sketch the whole first, then divide into parts; change a part only if doing so improves the whole. Never improve a part for its own sake.
  - Evaluate units (departments, services, pipeline stages) by contribution to the whole, not by their own local metrics.
  - "I've never seen a problem that couldn't be better solved somewhere other than where it was noticed." The team that spots a symptom (marketing sees a sales dip) claims the problem and solves it with its own levers — often the wrong place.
  - Disciplines are a filing system for knowledge, not a map of reality; explore several viewpoints and choose the one (or mix) giving the best solution. Nobody owns a problem.
  - In idea sessions, ban "why it won't work"; require "what would make it work". Premature criticism silences the outsider who holds the reframe.
  - Learning is driven by motivation and need; teaching is the best way to learn. Change in a field tends to come from outsiders (the four most-honoured US statisticians had never taken a statistics course).
- Failure modes
  - Divide-and-conquer management: optimise each function separately and the whole degrades.
  - Expert framing lock-in (the elevator engineers had three answers, all within their frame).
  - "Interdisciplinarity" bolted onto separate disciplines can't reassemble the whole (Humpty Dumpty); redesign from the whole instead.
  - Exams that reward the expected answer kill creativity, which by definition is the unexpected answer.
- Relevance to Sam
  - Pipelines: optimising each stage (fastest GeoNames parse, fastest matcher) can make the end-to-end pipeline worse if formats, chunk sizes or memory peaks don't fit on a 16GB machine. Judge stage changes by end-to-end throughput, correctness and memory.
  - Supplier integrations: "best" supplier per component (cheapest rates from one, best content from another) may not fit into a coherent booking flow; fit beats local best.
  - Before building a fix for an incident, ask whether it can be dissolved: e.g. instead of cleaning bad location rows every run, change the upstream ingest contract so bad rows can't enter; instead of warning users, redesign the flow (matchbook striker on the back).
  - Reframe user complaints (elevator): slow booking search may be solvable by perceived-speed design (progressive results, skeletons) instead of infra spend.
  - For Tereka and Riselist: the behaviour and incentives reveal real purpose; check whether the business model pays you for the outcome the customer wants.

## Donella Meadows — A Philosophical Look at System Dynamics (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XL_lOoomRTA)
**System dynamics models how a system changes over time by representing it as causal links that close into feedback loops around stocks (levels) and flows (rates); structure generates behaviour, so you read behaviour to infer structure and vice versa.**

- Mental models / first principles
  - The method's assumptions are like the lenses of your eyes: you see through them, not them. Four pillars: causal linkages, feedback loops, rates and levels, and the structure-behaviour link.
  - Dynamic, not static: the interesting object is a graph with time on the x-axis. Static snapshots (one year, cross-country) can't explain trajectories.
  - Causal, not correlational: every arrow should name a real-world mechanism by which A changes B. Doctors per capita and calories per capita correlate across 1971 countries, but neither causes the other; a third factor (industrial development) drives both. Correlation links are usable but are hypotheses to be proven.
  - Closed-loop worldview vs open-loop: the political/engineering habit is "push input, get output" (build roads, cut congestion). System dynamics always asks how B feeds back to change A — the invisible information link that changes future decisions.
  - Basic loop: state of the system -> (information, compared with a goal) -> decision/action -> changes the state. Jay Forrester's claim: no human decision is made outside a feedback loop.
  - State-determined systems: the system's own states drive its change; so look inside the system for the causes of its problems, not at outside shocks. This assumption fits many systems but not all — judging fit is part of the craft; if it doesn't fit, use another method.
- Mechanisms
  - Nonlinearity is the norm: food per capita and health services vs life expectancy rise steeply then saturate; more fertiliser or hospitals hit diminishing returns; allocation shares change as societies get richer. Draw a squiggle on nonlinear arrows; model them as table functions.
  - Delays matter for dynamics: ag investment -> food (years: land, irrigation, soil), births -> high mortality age (~70 years via age structure), health services -> life expectancy (vaccination build-out), food -> recovery of a malnourished child (months). Which delays you include depends on the model's time horizon (fridge time matters for a one-week family-diet model, not a 100-year model). Decide the horizon first.
  - Loop polarity: count negative links — odd = negative (balancing), even incl. zero = positive (reinforcing). Or reason it through: a balancing loop counteracts change toward a goal (beer-glass filling: gap -> open spigot -> level rises -> close); a reinforcing loop amplifies (bank interest: balance -> interest -> balance).
  - Multi-loop example (oil exploration): falling fraction of reserves remaining -> higher exploration cost -> higher price -> revenue -> exploration investment -> discovery -> less remaining (reinforcing overall). Embedded balancing loop: higher cost -> less oil found per dollar. Industry argument ("let prices rise and we'll find more") is right while the reinforcing loop dominates and wrong once depletion makes the balancing loop dominate. Shifts in loop dominance drive regime changes.
  - Levels and rates: a level (stock) is what decision makers look at; a rate (flow, drawn as a valve) is the decision. Solid arrows = material flows; dashed = information. Clouds = sources/sinks outside the boundary. Auxiliaries break a rate equation into steps. Every feedback loop should contain at least one level and one rate.
  - Structure -> behaviour theorems: a lone reinforcing loop gives exponential growth or decline; a balancing loop gives goal-seeking (exponential approach to goal from either side); reinforcing + balancing without delays gives S-shaped growth; add delays and you get overshoot, oscillation and more complex modes. Beyond a few loops intuition fails — simulate.
- Rules of thumb / checklists
  - When you hear "A causes B", ask how B comes back to influence A.
  - Something growing exponentially -> find the reinforcing loop. Something stuck that won't move despite pushing -> find the strong balancing loop pulling it back to an (often implicit) goal.
  - The hard part of modelling is not where to start but where to stop; set the boundary and time horizon explicitly.
  - Representing reality correctly beats following method rules; break the rules when the world demands it.
  - Model errors: when the model behaves wrongly, use structure-behaviour knowledge to locate the faulty loop.
- Failure modes
  - Linear assumptions ("more X always gives proportionally more Y").
  - Assuming instantaneous effects where real delays exist — gets the dynamics wrong (oscillation, overshoot).
  - Taking correlated variables as causal.
  - Open-loop policy thinking that ignores how the system reacts.
- Relevance to Sam
  - Stocks and flows in his systems: backlog of unmatched location rows (stock) with ingest rate in and match/clean rate out; booking pipeline queue; Riselist creator roster (recruit in, churn out); Tereka users. Ask which stock decision makers look at and which valve they control.
  - Reinforcing loops: content -> traffic -> bookings -> revenue -> more content (Drive Button); creator results -> referrals -> more creators. Balancing loops that will eventually dominate: supplier API rate limits, laptop RAM, the "easy matches first" depletion (like oil: remaining unmatched rows get progressively costlier to match — expect diminishing returns per hour of effort).
  - Delays: supplier data refresh lags, SEO lag, payment settlement lag — reacting to the visible state without accounting for the delay causes overcorrection.
  - Nonlinearity: throughput vs worker count saturates (memory, I/O); plan with saturation in mind.
  - Stuck metric despite effort? Look for a hidden goal/balancing loop (e.g. a team quietly treating "good enough" as the target).

## Dave Snowden (with Imre Porkoláb) — Discussion on the Cynefin framework, TEDxBudapestSalon (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_waoADNcaBU)
**Different kinds of systems (ordered, complex, chaotic) need different ways of acting; in complex systems you cannot engineer outcomes, you can only manage constraints, catalysts and where you put energy, then amplify good patterns and dampen bad ones.**

- Mental models / first principles
  - Sense-making = making sense of the world in order to act in it. Three natural system types, distinguished by constraint:
    - Ordered: high constraint, behaviour predictable; best/good practice works.
    - Complex: everything entangled; no repetition, high uncertainty, but patterns emerge that you can respond to.
    - Chaotic: no effective constraints; effectively random.
  - Transitions between them are phase shifts (like latent heat when water becomes steam): crossing a boundary takes, or releases, energy. A system is in one state or another, not halfway.
  - Complexity is modulated, not driven. "Driver" is cause-and-effect language. Think of magnets that change strength and polarity and shape what happens between them. You can't change the system directly; you change the modulators.
  - Creativity and outcomes live in the interactions between people, not in individuals. Leaders' common mistake: focus on individuals rather than linkages. It is more ethical (and more feasible) to change interactions than to try to change people.
- The children's party (method in miniature)
  - Chaotic approach: no constraints — the house burns down.
  - Ordered approach (satirised): learning objectives, mission posters, project plan with milestones, incentives tied to targets, after-action review into a best-practice database. Treats a complex system as ordered.
  - Complex approach: (1) set a boundary constraint ("don't cross that line"), ideally flexible and negotiable since rigid boundaries snap when least expected; (2) introduce catalytic probes (football, barbecue, video game) and see if a pattern of play — an attractor — forms; (3) attractors can be catalysed but not created; feed energy to beneficial ones, disrupt harmful ones (fire hoses). Sequence matters: boundaries, then catalysts, then energy allocation. Those are the only things you can manage in a complex adaptive system.
- Mechanisms / methods
  - Weak-signal detection (DARPA work): signals of a coming shift (e.g. Northern Ireland trust collapse) are present beforehand but noticed too late. After the fact (Pearl Harbor, Cuba, 9/11) the signals look obvious; at the time their volume is overwhelming. Approach: collect many micro-stories from the ground and visualise them so decision makers see people thinking differently before they conform.
  - Abductive logic: deduction (if A then B), induction (all As had B, so likely causal), abduction (most plausible link between unconnected things). Complexity work relies on abduction, which needs many perspectives to test whose intuitive leap is better.
  - Commander's intent via metaphor/counterfactual: a short reference both sides understand deeply (e.g. a specific battle episode) can convey in three words what 15 pages can't, enabling delegation in the field.
  - Leaders decide in political context, surrounded by people who share their blind spots; knowing something is right doesn't make it politically possible. Feed data accordingly.
  - Cognitive science of innovation: people mostly run on autonomic processing (habit) and switch to costly novelty-receptive processing only when habits fail. Biases are energy-saving heuristics and won't go away. Innovation needs the old way to become impossible: three conditions — starvation (scarce resources), pressure (tight time, with a safety net), and perspective shift (exposure to unfamiliar experts with no presuppositions).
  - Cognitively diverse trios: e.g. a fast young prototyper + a systems architect/tester + a user trained to talk to IT. Run many trios in parallel on the same problem; friction across viewpoints surfaces diversity cheaply.
  - Roles/crews over individuals (military crews, operating theatres): train in role and role expectations so performance doesn't depend on who fills the role. Humans need two to three years to co-evolve with key knowledge assets; apprenticeship can't be fully automated away.
- Decisions & trade-offs
  - Don't declare everything complex. Order is a human achievement and valuable. Key question: have we got stable enough constraints for predictable practice (use best/good practice) or is it truly complex (use parallel safe-to-fail experiments, diverse trios, networks)? Existing methods stay valid within their boundary; do something different beyond it.
  - Recipe followers vs chefs: recipes (consultancy playbooks) work only with the right ingredients and kit; chefs combine practice (embodied skill) and theory so they adapt to what's available. Build chefs via apprenticeship.
  - Facilitation: when the facilitator is on the floor, participants follow the facilitator's cultural expectations; direct indirectly.
- Failure modes
  - Applying ordered-system tools (targets, milestones, best-practice databases) to complex situations.
  - Countering a narrative attractor by repeating its keywords — re-triggers the same pattern and strengthens it.
  - Presenting only how terrible a big problem is leads people to deny it; small solvable actions shift awareness (plastic-waste-at-festivals project).
  - Rigid boundaries that break suddenly.
  - Signals visible but unheeded until a threshold flips the system overnight.
- Relevance to Sam
  - Sort work by domain: GeoNames transforms, deduplication rules and GTFS parsing are largely ordered (write the recipe, automate, test). TikTok creator virality, Tereka's market adoption, a new supplier's undocumented API behaviour are complex: run several small safe-to-fail probes in parallel, watch for attractors (what users/creators gravitate to), amplify, kill the rest. A live outage is chaotic: act first to stabilise, then sense.
  - Product management via constraints and catalysts: e.g. for Riselist set boundaries (brand rules, payment terms), seed catalysts (briefs, sample hooks), then allocate energy to the formats that take off rather than predefining a plan.
  - Weak signals: collect raw user/creator/customer stories and error anecdotes continuously; aggregate before they become an incident or churn wave.
  - Trios: pair a fast prototyper (often Sam + AI), someone who thinks about architecture/reliability, and a real user voice when designing new booking or planner features.

## Derek Cabrera — How Thinking Works, TEDxWilliamsport (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=dUqRTWCdXt4)
**Thinking is structuring information so you can do something with it, and it rests on four universal moves — Distinctions, Systems (part/whole), Relationships, Perspectives (DSRP) — which can be taught and practised directly.**

- Mental models / first principles
  - Cabrera's Ivy League students aced any structured test or assignment but struggled with unstructured tasks and novel problems: they had become good at "doing school", full of recall but unable to turn information into usable knowledge.
  - Thinking = taking information, structuring and organising it, and doing something meaningful with it. Creative, critical, systems, interdisciplinary/scientific and pro-social thinking all build on the same underlying moves.
  - Lego metaphor: old Lego came in a bucket with no instructions (you could build anything); modern kits come with step-by-step manuals. The thinking happens at the designer's desk, not the child's. Over-engineered curricula (and over-specified processes) remove the thinking from the person doing the work.
  - "Bandwidth solution" fallacy: pumping more information through the pipe and hoping thinking emerges ("then a miracle occurs"). You need a method, not more content.
  - Change comes bottom-up: ~60 million lessons a day are where education actually happens; fixes must reach that level rather than trickle down.
- Mechanism: DSRP
  - Distinctions: define what a thing is and is not; over time make finer, more nuanced distinctions. Drawing a distinction brings an idea into existence and sharpens communication.
  - Systems (part/whole): every part is a whole and every whole is a part. Be a "splumper": able to split (decompose) and lump (compose) fluidly.
  - Relationships: actively look for connections between ideas; schooling by department and discipline teaches disconnection.
  - Perspectives: deliberately view from other points of view; correlated with empathy, negotiation, conflict resolution and spatial reasoning.
  - These combine into complex structures; even a 10-month-old sorting Cheerios and berries is doing DSRP before having words for them. We are wired for it; rote, right-answer, no-mistakes schooling erodes it.
- Rules of thumb / checklist (apply to any messy problem)
  - D: What exactly is this thing, and what is it not? Are two things I'm treating as the same actually different (or vice versa)?
  - S: What are its parts? What larger whole is it a part of? Can I regroup the parts differently?
  - R: What is connected to what, and how? What relationship am I missing?
  - P: How does this look from the user's, the supplier's, the operator's, the finance view?
- Failure modes
  - Over-instructed environments produce people who can follow steps but not handle novelty.
  - More information without structuring tools overwhelms rather than informs. "If we don't teach them to think, others will happily think for them."
- Relevance to Sam
  - When using AI to build, the risk is the Lego-kit trap: the AI does the structuring and Sam follows instructions. Keep the DSRP moves on his own desk — define entities precisely (what counts as a "location", a "terminal", a "duplicate"), decompose and recompose pipelines, map relationships between datasets, and view designs from supplier/user/ops perspectives.
  - Data matching is literally a distinctions problem (is this place the same as that place?) and a part/whole problem (city within region within country); DSRP is a useful review checklist for schemas.
  - For teams or contractors (Riselist creators, Trameter colleagues), give boundaries and goals rather than over-specified step lists if you want them to think.

## Russell Ackoff — Beyond Continual Improvement (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xMFLTcUP44E)
**Most quality/improvement programmes fail because they are anti-systemic: they improve parts separately, they chase removal of defects rather than what is wanted, and they confuse continuous improvement with the discontinuous, creative leaps that actually create leadership.**

- Mental models / first principles
  - Surveys he cites: over two-thirds of managers who authorised quality/improvement programmes judged them failures. Since quality means meeting the customer's expectations and the manager is the customer of the programme, that verdict stands regardless of experts' criteria.
  - Same system definition as his longer talk: a whole that can't be divided into independent parts; essential properties belong to the whole (a car moves you; the motor can't even move itself); a system is the product of its parts' interactions, not the sum of their actions.
- Three systemic principles ignored by improvement programmes
  1. Improving parts separately does not improve the whole (best-parts car doesn't even run). Architect principle: design the whole first, and change a room only if the house is simultaneously improved.
  2. Getting rid of what you don't want doesn't give you what you do want. Defect-hunting traces back to Shewhart's 1930s statistical defect detection at Bell Labs. TV example: if the chance the current channel shows what you want is ~0.01, switching channel removes a "defect" but leaves you with the same ~0.01 chance. Direct improvement at what you want, not at what you don't.
  3. Idealised redesign: decide what you would do right now if you could do anything you wanted; without knowing that, you can't know what's best under constraints.
- Decisions & trade-offs
  - Continuous improvement vs creativity: creativity is a discontinuity that breaks with what came before. Continuous improvement is imitation; you don't overtake a leader by copying and slightly improving, you leapfrog through creativity.
  - Efficiency vs effectiveness (Drucker's doing things right vs doing the right thing): Japanese carmakers raised car quality (efficiency) while the car itself degrades cities (congestion, pollution keeping children indoors) — "the wrong kind of quality". Quality must include value, not just conformance. Efficiency maps to knowledge; effectiveness to wisdom.
- Rules of thumb
  - Before an improvement programme, ask: is this improving a part or the whole? Is it targeting a defect or a desired outcome? Have I designed the ideal before optimising under constraints?
  - Judge success by the customer's (requester's) expectations, not the practitioner's preferred metric.
- Failure modes
  - Local optimisation, defect-removal as strategy, and incrementalism as a substitute for redesign.
- Relevance to Sam
  - Pipeline quality work: removing error classes (nulls, bad coords) doesn't by itself produce a dataset users want; define the target output (e.g. "every bookable destination matched to a canonical location with transport access") and work backward.
  - Idealised design for Trameter booking: sketch the booking flow Sam would build if supplier constraints didn't exist (the in-house booking aim), then work back to what's feasible with current Viator/RateHawk access.
  - For side businesses, incremental polish (another planner feature, another agency template) rarely beats a discontinuous reframe of the offer.
