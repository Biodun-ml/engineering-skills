# startup-1 — cross-cutting principles

1. **PMF is unmistakable pull, not interest.** Customers rip the product out of your hands: every metric jumps at once, people ask "how do we start?", "wait, you can do *what*?". If you're asking whether you have it, you don't. (Reinhardt/Segment; Snyder)
2. **Pull lives in one person with a blocked, urgent project.** PULL = Project on their to-do list + Unavoidable now + List of options they've considered + Limitations that leave them stuck. All four must be true. Without U they complain and don't buy. With good-enough options they do it themselves. (Snyder)
3. **Idle interest and politeness are noise.** "Great, I understand the value", "send more info", "maybe Q4", pity-trials, website chats and design partners who "play Steve Jobs" are all no. If you feel like you're pushing or convincing, that's a red flag. (Reinhardt; Snyder)
4. **Ideas come from first-hand reality, not brainstorming.** Deliberately brainstormed ideas are "bad and plausible." Get to the leading edge of a field, work on what interests you, embed with or shadow the user, or solve a problem you'd have *paid* to have solved. (Graham "Before the Startup"; Snyder: ~90% of successes came from first-hand immersion)
5. **Expertise in your users beats expertise in startups. Don't play house or hunt for tricks.** Users are like sharks: meat or no meat. Press, hiring, conferences and fundraising are sizzle. Shipping and talking to users is the steak. (Graham; Seibel "Mistakes"; Caldwell & Seibel)
6. **Launch something bad quickly.** MVP in weeks, not months; under a month for most consumer/B2B. Time-box the spec, write it down, cut it. Separate "first customers" launch from press launch. Heavy MVPs (regulated, hard tech) still start with an explanatory website. (Seibel "MVP"; Seibel "Mistakes")
7. **Hold the user and problem tight, the solution loose.** When it fails, fix the screwdriver, don't hunt for new uses. Be explicit about whether you're sliding the *product* or the *market*, never both silently. (Seibel "MVP"; Reinhardt)
8. **One feature that works beats many that don't.** Avoid "Google-itis" (copying today's giant). Amazon = books, Gusto = payroll only, OpenSea = buy/sell with a third-party wallet, Airbnb = no payments. (Caldwell & Seibel; Seibel "MVP")
9. **Do things that don't scale to land and learn.** Stripe integrated in customers' offices. Service-shaped delivery (humans plus automation) is fine early. "Goblin mode" (fly to a conference, go door-to-door) when stuck. The "Hail Mary" question: "anything on your list you'd pay me to do right now?" (Seibel "MVP"; Snyder; Graham)
10. **Kill bad ideas fast and run cheap ("cockroach") until PMF.** No spend on sales or marketing before fit. Short test cycles (~5 calls per hypothesis). Codecademy tried ~12 ideas in 7 weeks. (Reinhardt; Snyder)
11. **Ask for money early and price from the alternatives.** Test willingness to transact first. Anchor price to the cost of the current option or the value of finishing the project. Ask high and let buyers negotiate (Segment asked $120k, got $18k, now >$100k deals). Lines of code have nothing to do with value. No proactive pilots, free POCs or design partners. (Snyder; Reinhardt)
12. **Validation from investors, press, peers or competitions is a driver's licence, not proof.** "It worked in country X" doesn't transfer PMF. Only customer ground truth counts. (Caldwell & Seibel)
13. **A foothold expands easily.** Once you solve one problem, customers bring adjacent ones ("5 of 5 customers hand-build the same S3→Redshift pipeline"). Tiny scope is fine. Your vision can come later. (Reinhardt)
14. **Retention: find the "X" moment after which churning is weird** and build onboarding as a bullet train to it. If most churn, find the segment that stays and go vertical and premium. (Snyder)
15. **A startup is one case study repeated.** Factory = pipeline → sales → delivery. Work only on the current bottleneck (theory of constraints). The case study doubles as a qualification tool. (Snyder)
16. **Shipping pace plus discipline are the controllable predictors.** AI hasn't changed most startup fundamentals. Discipline outweighs raw intelligence. Act now with "12 months of runway" urgency. Stage spending to milestones; starting cheap still works. (Graham "Ambition"; Caldwell & Seibel)
17. **Founder fit and people.** Pick a problem and users you'll care about for 5+ years (Twitch only worked once a gamer served gamers). Pick co-founders you know. Trust your instincts about people. Hold regular open talks on performance, goals and roles. (Seibel "Mistakes"; Graham)
18. **First users should be nameable today.** Yourself, people you know, or a list you can reach. Cold outreach: a short "you're interesting, I'm interesting, let's chat" hook. Benchmarks ~30–40% LinkedIn accept, 30–50% reply, cold email low single digits. Expect slammed doors. (Seibel; Snyder; Reinhardt)
19. **Don't take public money for what you can't yet deliver.** Crowdfunding or presales at scale before you can build is a "one-shot kill" reputational risk. (Caldwell & Seibel)
20. **Attack incumbents when the paradigm shifts, not head-on.** Copying a clear winner with no theory of an unblocked customer is a tarpit. (Graham "Ambition"; Snyder)

**For Sam specifically:**
- Write a PULL card for Tereka (who has an *unavoidable* money deadline this month?) and for Riselist (which brand marketer is blocked, and what do they pay now?).
- Ship time-boxed MVPs with analytics.
- Hand-deliver to the first users.
- Concentrate on the one venture with real pull rather than spreading across many.

## Rob Snyder — How to find product-market fit (but, actually) | Lenny's Podcast (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=19qrLchbQEc)
**Pull is a property of a specific person who is stuck on an urgent project their current options can't finish; find it, don't manufacture it, then repeat one case study forever.**

- **Mental model / first principle.** "Pain points", "value props" and "design partners using it daily" did not produce sales for Snyder's first startup (two years of pivoting). People can agree your product solves their pain and still not reorder their priorities to buy it. What makes someone rip a product out of your hands is a *current priority they are blocked on*. Such a person is a "coiled spring"; buying is the obvious move, so it would be *weird for them not to buy*. Most prospects are the opposite: it would be weird if they bought.
- **The PULL acronym (all four must be present at once):**
  - **P – Project**: something on their (mental) to-do list, phrased as a job ("hire more people"), not "buy recruiting software". Can be an intention attached to a recurring workflow ("spend the least time possible on CRM notes").
  - **U – Unavoidable**: why now and not later; a deadline, regulation, boss, consequence.
  - **L – List of options** they consider: hire someone, do it in-house, existing tools, competitors, doing nothing.
  - **L – Limitations**: why each option fails for them, so they are actually stuck.
  - Diagnosis when one is missing: P without U and without failing L's → they complain but never buy. P+U but good-enough L's → "we're already doing that ourselves with Claude Code".
- **Worked example (Jump, AI notetaker for financial advisors).** P: minimise time writing client-meeting notes into the CRM. U: regulators require the notes (legal exposure). L: generic AI notetakers. Limitations: no integration with advisor CRMs, unclear SEC compliance. Result: extreme pull. **Counter-example:** insurance agents have the same P but no U (notes aren't required), so their existing option, not taking notes, is fine. No pull.
- **Pull belongs to a person, not a company.** You sell to "the head of legal at X who has project Y", not to "mid-market PE firms".
- **Idea generation.** Product ideas from a notebook all sound plausible, and you can't tell good from bad. The pull hypothesis sits upstream of the product: "I believe this person is stuck on X right now." Of ~50 startups he helped to $1M+ ARR, ~10% of ideas came from founders' own experience and ~90% from going and getting first-hand exposure: shadowing, embedding, doing the job. One founder shadowed several small-business verticals for days each (dropped solar installers, landed on independent physical therapists) and even looked at buying a franchise to see the P&L. "Earned secrets" come from reality, not whiteboards. First-hand exposure also matters because you'll have to make thousands of decisions for that customer later.
- **"Solve your own problem" test.** It works only if you *would have paid* someone to solve it at the time. "The world should have this" is not pull.
- **Validation artifact: two slides.** Slide 1 states their pull in plain words: "You're trying to do X now because of Y; you looked at A/B but they fail because Z. Did I get that right?" Slide 2 gives a conceptual sketch of how you'd help. You want "oh my god, yes" on slide 1 and "how does it work / how do we start?" on slide 2. Then check the same hypothesis with ~5 more people so you aren't building for one person.
- **Signal vs noise.** Real signal is a *pull-shaped action*: "how do we get started?", "if you can do that I'll pay you right now" (said by a pharma exec before any product existed). Noise is leaned-back enthusiasm: "interesting, send more info", "could see this in Q4", "this is the future of our industry". If you feel like you're *pushing or convincing*, treat it as a red flag.
- **Hail Mary.** When a call goes lukewarm, ask: "Is there anything on your to-do list you'd pay me to take off your plate right now?" Several startups pivoted on the answer. Example: a gov ATS startup's first contract was a job-description problem the buyer raised in reply.
- **Getting first calls cold ("founder magic" outreach).** One or two sentences that make you interesting, with no pain-point pitch. Examples: "Snoop Dogg invested in us, thought you'd find it interesting, open to chat?" or "I worked at my family's plumbing firm and now I'm building X". Fit the hook to the audience (an HVAC owner won't care about Silicon Valley credentials). He has not seen AI personalisation work; it reads as machine-written. LinkedIn benchmarks: connect with ~20/day (100–150/week); 30–40% should accept; 30–50% of those should reply; most repliers should agree to talk. Low accept rate points at your profile. Low reply rate points at your message. Target 2–3 customer conversations per day.
- **Sales call structure (30-min invite, since shorter slots get skipped).** Open with "Why did you take this call?" and they may fill in the whole PULL for you. Otherwise turn what they said into an agreed agenda, then *earn the right* to ask discovery questions ("before we get to that, mind if I ask a few questions about your context?") so it doesn't feel like an interrogation. A PULL-shaped question: "Is there anything around your QA process you're trying to do but can't with your current tools, methods or team?" Then probe the scary ones: "Is it OK if this waits?" and "Are your current options good enough?" Keep a doc open and fill in P/U/L/L live. Once pull is surfaced, keep the pitch *small*: restate their need and one simple way you'd meet it. No grand vision.
- **Iteration loop.** Run ~5 calls on a hypothesis, then review. Zero pull and nothing learned means change the hypothesis. Use intros from the "not for me" calls; one team's banking calls led to an intro into defense, where the very first call had pull. "Sell to learn, not to push." Emergency mode ("goblin mode"): get on a plane, book 20 meetings at a conference, have family go door-to-door to dental offices. Being stuck is the costliest state.
- **10x better?** He rejects "10x" as the test. If alternatives *don't get the job done*, you are infinitely better. If the buyer isn't blocked, 100x better still won't sell ("a 100x better home printer I won't buy"). His own failure: better UX in HR software, but UX wasn't relevant to the buyer's project.
- **Closing.** Come back within days with a concept or mockup (vibe-coded is fine), then ask "anything else you need to decide?" **Pricing bounds come from the L's**: value of finishing the project vs what the alternatives cost (consultants at $50k/yr suggests ~$50k/yr). Early on, test *willingness to transact* more than willingness to pay. To anchor high safely, present a range of case-study prices ("some did a small project at 80k, some up to 1M, most land in the middle, how are you thinking about it?") so both of you react to an example instead of you proposing. For big orgs, ask the champion "when must this show results?" and work backwards to set the procurement steps.
- **No design partners, pilots or free POCs offered proactively.** Design partners "play Steve Jobs" and never buy. People who truly have pull don't want to co-build; they want the thing done. Offering a partnership repels real buyers and attracts tourists. If their procurement *requires* a pilot, fine.
- **Service-shaped start is fine.** Sell the outcome, delivered partly by humans and partly automated, while you learn what to build. The risk is getting stuck as a low-margin consultancy, but every path has downsides.
- **Retention.** Assume every customer churns until they hit "X", the moment after which churning would be weird. X is rarely usage or NPS. It's the leading indicator. For his restaurant-retention product, X was getting a preventable "employee about to quit" alert in the first two weeks. Design onboarding as a "bullet train" to X and deliver early accounts by hand. With 90%+ churn on a horizontal product, find the 1–2 segments that stay (one AI-workflow tool found freight forwarders using one workflow) and pivot into a premium vertical product.
- **Case study factory (his "most important" idea).** A startup is one case study ("they were trying to do X, options failed because Y, we unblocked them with Z") repeated as often as possible. The factory has three stations: **pipeline** (getting conversations), **sales** (turning conversations into customers) and **delivery** (making churn weird). Apply theory of constraints and work only on the current bottleneck. The case study also serves as a qualification tool ("you don't look like our case study, this may be a lot of work").
- **Tarpit he sees most:** copying a clear winner with no theory of which customer *they* leave blocked.
- **Relevance to Sam.**
  - *Tereka*: write a consumer PULL. Who has an *unavoidable* money project right now (rent due in 2 weeks, school fees, ajo/esusu contribution, salary week)? Are notebooks, bank apps, PiggyVest-type savings or WhatsApp groups their options, and why do they fail? If no U shows up, expect "nice app" and no usage.
  - *Riselist*: service-first is explicitly endorsed. Ask the Hail Mary on every brand call, and price against what the brand currently spends (agencies, influencer fees).
  - *Trameter*: with any supplier or B2B partner, name the person and their project. Treat Drive Button "X" (first booking click within N days?) as the retention trigger.
  - *Solo, two jobs*: 5-call cycles and LinkedIn at 20/day fit evening hours. Watch for "pushing".

## Peter Reinhardt (Segment) — How to Find Product Market Fit, Stanford CS183F (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_6pl5GG8RQ4)
**Product-market fit is unmistakable. It feels like a landmine going off, not a glimmer of interest. If you have to ask whether you have it, you don't.**

- **First principle.** Companies are not founded by imagining the future and building toward it (Alan Kay's research method). Founding is *inductive*: you go back and forth with customers until something works. Founders can easily build something that *looks* futuristic. The hard part is solving a specific problem people have *today*. "The market always wins" over your vision. Figure ~80% of founders never find PMF.
- **Why it's hard.** You want PMF so badly that you see mirages. Reinhardt's ML analogy: Segment had many *negative* training examples and *zero positive* ones, so it couldn't classify signals. A study he cites: failing once doesn't raise your odds next time (~20–22%), but succeeding once raises them to ~34%. The useful information is in knowing what success *felt* like, which is why he tells stories.
- **Phases.** (1) "Cockroach": build, launch and kill several ideas while minimising burn. Founders paid themselves the legal minimum (~$20–30k), lived in the office, and spent nothing on sales or marketing before PMF. (2) PMF hits. (3) Everything becomes *easier* (customers buy, hires join) though not easy. Jason Lemkin's SaaS ladder: $0–1M is impossible (PMF), $1–10M improbable (a brutal grind with a skeleton crew because great execs won't join yet), $10–100M inevitable (momentum). Category leaders end up 10–1000x bigger than the next competitor and become *platforms* that others build on (Salesforce AppExchange, Amazon sellers). Thiel's figure: ~$100M revenue before a platform is viable, so that 2–3% of your base is a real business for a partner.
- **Failure story 1: ClassMetric (lecture-confusion button).** Hundreds of thousands of lines of code, a $600k raise, a big "future classroom" vision. Professors tried it *out of pity*. Standing at the back of the class showed that no student had the tool open; laptops became distractions. **"Bullying customers into using your product is not PMF."** They offered investors their money back; most re-invested in the team.
- **Failure story 2: own analytics product.** Did "Lean Startup" coffee chats and got *idle interest*: "keep me posted", willingness to meet, a stray 3am website chat. They coded for six months with no customers (wrong order of operations). Prospects were happy with Mixpanel/GA and asked only for edge-case features, which Reinhardt mistook for a wide gap. Paul Graham's verdict after $500k: "you have nothing to show for it." They had $100k left.
- **Success story: analytics.js.** A ~100-line (later 580-line) internal library that translated one tracking call into many analytics APIs. They first used it as a *sales objection handler* ("try us alongside Mixpanel"). People loved the library and ignored the product, which was the signal they missed. Reinhardt tried to *kill* the idea with a landing page on Hacker News. It went to #1 (~300 upvotes), got thousands of GitHub stars and ~10 PRs in 48 hours, and people begged on LinkedIn for beta access, asking for 10 more integrations and saying they'd deploy tomorrow. *Every metric went haywire at once.* The customer runs ahead and you're saying "wait, it's not ready." No vision was attached; it solved the tiniest real problem. The tiny product was a *foothold*.
- **Why anyone paid for open source.** The real problem was organisational. Marketing kept buying tools and asking engineering to implement each one, which wrecked roadmaps. The hosted version let engineers instrument *once* and marketers toggle destinations. The value of a business problem has *nothing to do with lines of code*.
- **Finding adjacent PMF later.** Expanding from a foothold is easier than finding the first problem, because customers bring you adjacent problems (especially in B2B). He saw many customers using the S3 integration and visited 5 large NY customers. 5 of 5 said their data engineers were turning S3 logs into CSV, managing schemas and loading Redshift. The identical answers made the build obvious (a Redshift/warehouse connector), and every metric jumped at launch. **Idea-test signal:** he pitched 5 ideas to a data architect. Four got "that's great, I understand the value" (*code for "doesn't care", just politeness*). The fifth got "wait, sorry, you can do *what*?" and the architect immediately set up meetings with other teams.
- **Rules of thumb.**
  - The problem isn't a shortage of ideas. It's failing to **kill bad ideas fast**. Codecademy tried ~12 ideas in ~7 weeks during YC and had a huge launch on the last idea.
  - **Ask for money early.** Segment *massively underpriced* a "small" product. A sales advisor made Reinhardt ask for $120k/yr; prospects negotiated to $18k, and eventually someone said "that seems reasonable." Customers now pay >$100k/yr. You don't know the value you deliver until you ask.
  - **Slide product OR market, and know which.** Changing audience and product at once lets you fool yourself. Be explicit about which variable you moved.
  - **Discovery script.** Don't pitch. Ask about their business problems: "Do you spend a lot on Facebook? How do you know it's efficient? Is that a problem? Why haven't you solved it?" Then dig. That sets up the sale, or tells you what to build.
  - **Finding first customers** is hustle: cold email (reply rates in low single digits), friends, investors, LinkedIn. "Sales is the door slammed nine times out of ten."
- **Failure modes.** Writing code before customers. Treating idle interest, sign-up trickles, or pity usage as fit. Measuring usage only by data you didn't instrument (they "should have known from analytics"). Shipping "one more feature" to reach PMF. The emotional toll is real: he lost 10 lbs and had panic attacks, so plan for it.
- **Relevance to Sam.** Tereka: watch for "the library people love while ignoring the product". Whichever tiny piece (a budget template, salary-split calculator, WhatsApp bot) gets shared or copied may be the foothold. Measure real use (do they come back on payday?), not compliments. Trameter: the "five of five customers do the same manual workaround" pattern fits supplier data. If several partners hand-build the same CSV/mapping, that's the product. Drive Button: test with a real public launch (HN, Product Hunt, Nigerian travel-blogger communities) and treat lukewarm sign-ups as no. Pricing: Sam's B2B work (Riselist brands, Gombella-style buyers) should ask high and let buyers negotiate down. Low-burn "cockroach" mode is his default setup (two jobs, laptop), and it buys him many shots.

## Paul Graham — Before the Startup (How to Start a Startup 2014, Lecture 3) (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f4_14pZlJBs)
**Startups are counterintuitive like skiing, so distrust your instincts about the work (but trust them about people). What wins is expertise in your users, not in startups.**

- **Mental model.** Your instincts give wrong answers in startups the way leaning back on skis speeds you up. Good advice will feel wrong, which is why founders ignore YC partners and regret it a year later. Remembering "my instinct may be wrong here" gives you a pause before the mistake.
- **The six counterintuitive points:**
  1. **Startups are counterintuitive.** Pause before acting on instinct about the business.
  2. **Trust your instincts about people.** Founders repeatedly ignore misgivings about someone impressive and regret it. Engineers tend to assume "slightly distasteful" is normal in business. It isn't. Pick co-founders and early hires like friends: people you like, respect, and have known long enough to see how they act when interests conflict.
  3. **You need expertise in your users, not in startups.** Zuckerberg was a startup noob (Facebook first incorporated as a Florida LLC) but understood his users. Learning startup mechanics can be *dangerous* because it leads to **"playing house"**: a plausible idea, a raise, a nice office, hiring friends, all while skipping the one essential thing, *making something people want*.
  4. **Gaming the system stops working.** School trains you to find the trick. PG studied by predicting the 20–30 exam-shaped ideas. Founders ask "how do I…" (convince investors, grow fast, growth hacks) and the answer starts with "just": make something users love, then tell people. Users are like sharks: meat or no meat. You can fake investors for a round or two, but you're only fooling yourself because your equity is in a doomed company. A founder with a great usage graph and no fundraising knowledge beats one who knows every trick and has a flat graph.
  5. **Startups are all-consuming.** If it succeeds, it takes years to a decade or more. Successful YC founders all say "it never gets easier"; the problems change but the total worry stays the same or grows. It's like having kids, an irreversible button. PG says don't do it at 20 in college. Your early 20s are for cheap exploration and serendipity, which also widens the pool you pick your life's work from.
  6. **You can't tell if you're up to it.** Startups change you. After nine years of judging founders, PG found he could gauge intelligence in 10 minutes but *not* future toughness or ambition. Swagger vs self-doubt had no correlation with outcome. Unless you're terrified, the only way to find out is to try.
- **How to get ideas: don't try to think of them.** Deliberate brainstorming produces ideas that are *bad and plausible*, so they fool you and everyone else. Instead turn your brain into one that has ideas unconsciously: (1) learn a lot about things that matter, (2) work on problems that interest you, (3) with people you like and respect, which also gets you co-founders. Yahoo, Google, Facebook and Apple all started as side projects. The best ideas are such outliers that your conscious mind would reject them as companies.
  - **Fractal-stain model.** Technology spreads like a stain, and every point on its moving edge is an interesting problem. Get to the leading edge ("live in the future", Paul Buchheit) and things that look prescient to others look obvious to you. Example: a Harvard grad student built his own VoIP in the mid-90s just to call his girlfriend in Taiwan for free.
  - Domain expertise is the part of "entrepreneurship" that matters. Larry Page was an expert on search out of curiosity. Add the ulterior motive (a startup) *at the end*. Two-word advice: **"just learn."**
  - Test of taste: if you can tolerate boring work (middle management, etc.), you either have huge discipline or no taste for interesting problems.
- **Q&A takeaways.**
  - **Non-technical founder:** in a domain startup (e.g. limo industry for Uber) they may do most of the work, such as recruiting supply. In a pure tech startup, they do sales and support the programmers.
  - **Business school:** mostly no. It teaches management, which you need only after success. Design school is more relevant. Learn by doing (PG retracted his old advice to work at a company first).
  - **Early hires** should be founder-like and need almost no managing. Airbnb took ~5 months to hire its first employee. Hiring friends risks monoculture, but the benefits outweigh it.
  - **Bubble vs high prices:** a bubble is knowingly buying to offload on a greater fool. High prices alone aren't one. Still, assume the next raise will be harder.
  - **When a side project becomes a startup:** when it takes over an alarming share of your life.
  - **Growing but not explosive?** Do things that don't scale.
  - **Facing funding bias:** make the growth graph so good it speaks first ("growth graphs have no gender").
- **Failure modes:** playing house, looking for tricks, ignoring warnings about people, starting too early by just going through the motions, brainstorming "plausible" ideas.
- **Relevance to Sam.** Sam is 28, has domain depth (GeoNames/OSM, GTFS, supplier APIs, Nigerian personal finance from lived experience) and lives at the AI leading edge. That is PG's "fractal edge" recipe. His strongest ideas will likely come from things he built for himself or for Trameter (a location-matching tool, a supplier mapper), not from a list. The "playing house" warning applies to Tereka, Riselist, the YouTube channel and grants all at once: branding, names and sites aren't progress unless users want the thing. The "all-consuming" warning with two jobs means at most one venture can be the real startup. The side-project test ("is it eating an alarming share of my time because I can't stop?") tells him which one.

## Paul Graham — On Startups, Ambition, and Great Founders (YC interview, 2026) (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=5bxp78i96S8)
**Most of starting a startup is always the same, even with AI. What changes outcomes is formidable, ambitious founders who ship fast, and you can still start cheap and stage your spending to milestones.**

- **Invariance principle.** In the 21st year and 47th batch of YC, PG says startup problems are the same whether the tech is microprocessors, combustion engines or AI. Asked what AI changed: "so far almost everything is exactly the same." The one new thing is cost structure. The big expense used to be salaries; now some startups spend tens of thousands of dollars a day on tokens.
- **Shipping speed is still the best predictor.** AI tools haven't removed the variation. Many startups in the current batch still ship too slowly, because the bottleneck includes *thinking of what to build*, not only producing it.
- **Ambition and what actually drives founders.** Ambition is required because the obstacles are too fearsome for mere talent. But the day-to-day motivator is not wanting to be a billionaire. It's *fear of failure*: the server crashing, looking foolish. His analogy is a model train falling off the table: you keep saving it for 10 years, look up, and you're rich. Ambition is mostly inborn. When it seems missing, the person was usually trained (school, pushy parents) *not to show it*.
- **Startups as credential = a category error.** A Harvard admission lets you coast through an easy major. A startup has no easy major, like being forced to study theoretical physics. A failed startup is a weak badge, and a successful one is your life's work, not a badge. To seem cool, it's the least efficient route, with years of grind first.
- **"Formidable" defined:** someone who *gets what they want* in any situation. Investors want them because they own part of what the founder gets.
- **Is lean dead? (vs Collison).** No. Technology keeps getting cheaper. Inference at a fixed quality falls dramatically, roughly 30x/yr by his estimate, and token prices hide quality gains. **Stage spending to milestones:** even a rocket company can start with a design, simulations and expert validation, raise for the next stage, and repeat. Example: StarCloud wrote a white paper, *booked a launch* and raised. A booked launch is a strong commitment signal, though the founder's expert credibility helped.
- **New Google via model shift.** In 2012 PG argued you don't beat Google head-on; you wait until its model is obsolete. LLMs did that: people wanted *information*, not web pages. Strategy rule: attack incumbents when the underlying paradigm changes, not on their own turf.
- **Idea pattern from the batch.** On-demand cancer research for individual patients, built by the people who did it for GitLab's Sid ("founder mode on my cancer"). Generalises to: *productise what an expert did for one person*. Also "death by a thousand cuts" (many small attacks) for problems with no single cure.
- **AI's shape.** Expected: perfect fly → mouse → human. Actual: a full human-level generalist that's unreliable, like a BS-ing undergrad, improving toward reliable. The "AGI finish line" turns out to be a *smear* with width: some capabilities are far past it, others (like getting restaurant hours right) are not. That's the jagged frontier. For builders, check reliability per task rather than assuming general competence.
- **Why batches/peers help.** Founding is lonely. A cohort gives colleagues, shared solutions to technical problems, and the "YC GDP": a built-in early-adopter market of fast-deciding peers who will at least hear your pitch.
- **YC's origin as a lesson.** It started as an "angel firm" (small, early, standardised paperwork). They funded many startups at once *to learn how to invest*, framed it as a summer-job alternative, and discovered the batch model by accident. Small experiment, keep what works.
- **Where the next giant company comes from:** the right founders more than a particular idea, since ideas are mutable. With 20 years of data, great founders look the same.
- **Relevance to Sam.**
  - With AI tools, Sam's edge is *pace of shipping plus choosing what to ship*. Track weekly shipped-to-users items per project.

## Dalton Caldwell & Michael Seibel — Simple Products That Became Big Companies (Rookie Mistakes) (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=G7TMqY7gkGY)
**A product with one feature that works beats a product with many features that don't. Start with one use case, get ground truth from users, and don't mistake approval from investors, press or peers for validation.**

- **Mistake 1: "boiling the ocean".** Serving several use cases before nailing one and proving that customer exists. Every founder starts with a *useful lie*: that they know what the customer wants and how to solve it. The more you build before users see it, the bigger the lie grows. Putting something in front of users tells you which hypotheses were true.
- **"Google-itis".** Copying today's Amazon/Google/Gusto feature set without seeing what they looked like at birth. Amazon was a simple bookstore site. **OpenSea** (YC 2018, after a pivot) spent ~1–2 years grinding on a barely working site that only let you buy and sell NFTs. It shipped daily or weekly and didn't build its own wallet (it used third-party ones). Simplicity plus focus got real users, then network effects, then the "overnight" billions. **Gusto** began as ZenPayroll doing only payroll, online, versus ADP sending someone to your office and a monthly phone call. Good payroll is the kind you forget about.
- **Core rule:** *a product with many broken features is infinitely worse than one feature that works.* Picture payroll + benefits + 401k where nothing works and money sometimes goes missing. That's how hated companies (the "Verizon" feeling) get made. Reliability of the core job comes before breadth.
- **Mistake 2: copying an overseas model and assuming PMF transfers.** "It works in country X so it'll work in Y." Investors like funding these because the thesis is simple. Founders then scale hiring, burn cash, and learn the users don't want it. Validation from investors, press, friends, employees or competition wins is a *driver's licence*, not proof you drive well. Nobody stops you until things go bad.
- **Why founders fall for authority.** School taught everything through a teacher (nobody rediscovers physics by dropping rocks). Startups require learning from first-hand observation of the problem. People who email Dalton and Michael asking "is my idea good?" have missed the point. Authority figures don't have the answer; only customers' ground truth does.
- **Investor-enabled failure pattern.** Raise big, ignore advisors for 2 years, hire 50, come back with "it's not working." Then the question is "did you solve anyone's problem? What's your revenue?" A real rationalisation they heard: "we must grow the number of customers who *could* buy before those who *do*."
- **Runway and discipline.** Decision quality improves sharply when runway hits ~12 months and magical thinking fades. Like a college all-nighter, you could have done that work all along. **Discipline beats genius:** for every ounce of discipline you can lose 5–10 ounces of intelligence and still win. People who out-execute peers often just *did the work*, while peers hired, went to conferences and talked to reporters. Focused work is trainable like exercise; it hurts less with practice. "Hard workers are dangerous". Lazy, distracted competitors are your friends.
- **Mistake 3: hardware crowdfunding before you can build.** Batch manufacturing economics (10,000 shirts cost less per unit than one) make preorders tempting. But early crowdfunders don't know build cost, right price, timeline, or whether it solves the problem, and they're holding lots of money and expectations. Missing delivery turns thousands of strangers into enemies ("scammer"). It can follow you forever (a permanent Wikipedia page), and intent doesn't matter to them. Best practice: crowdfund at v2/v3 when you're ready to manufacture at scale. The general rule covers crypto and any money taken from the public at scale: *you will be held accountable*. It's a "one-shot kill" risk. When everyone in your sector does X, that doesn't mean X works (the crowdfunding fad lasted ~6–7 years).
- **Checklist.**
  - Name the single use case and the user who has it.
  - Ship the simplest working version and iterate weekly.
  - Use third-party components for non-core pieces (OpenSea's wallet).
  - Measure revenue and problem-solved, not press or funding.
  - Act now with the urgency you'd have at 12 months of runway.
  - Don't pre-sell to the public what you can't yet deliver.
- **Relevance to Sam.**
  - *Tereka*: one job done reliably (e.g. split this month's salary into cups and remind me on payday) beats a feature-rich planner. A money app that's ever wrong about money is the "Verizon" outcome. Copying a US/UK budgeting app into Nigeria is exactly Mistake 2. Validate with Nigerian users first.
  - *Trameter booking*: integrate suppliers (Viator, RateHawk) rather than building inventory. Make one booking flow bulletproof before adding categories.
  - *Riselist*: never take creator or brand money upfront for results you can't yet deliver.
  - *Grants*: winning one is a driver's licence, not PMF.
  - *Two jobs*: discipline and protected focused blocks are his main lever.

## Michael Seibel — How to Plan an MVP (YC Startup School) (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=1hHMwLxN6EM)
**An MVP is the first ridiculously simple thing you can hand to your first target users to see if you deliver any value. Launch something bad quickly, then iterate on the solution while holding the user and problem fixed.**

- **Definition.** Strip the jargon. The MVP is the first thing your initial users can touch to test whether you can deliver *any* value. It's a base to iterate from, nothing special. A few user conversations first help (no multi-year research needed). Being your own user is best because you can tell if it works.
- **"How do I get first users?" is a smell.** If you chose a problem you know someone has, your first user is *that person*. If you can't name your users, question the idea.
- **Pre-launch goals, in order:** (1) launch quickly, (2) get *any* customers using it (many founders never get one user to touch their product), (3) talk to users and get feedback on the crappy version, (4) iterate. Founders discount feedback on the "small thing" because the "full thing" needs 3 years and $10M. But the full vision may not be what customers want.
- **Hold tight / hold loose:** hold the *problem* and the *customer* tightly. Hold the *solution* loosely.
- **Iterate vs pivot (screwdriver parable).** When the screwdriver fails the mechanic, founders look for new uses for the screwdriver (cooking? cleaning?). Wrong. Keep the mechanic and the need to drive a screw, and *fix the screwdriver*. Falling in love with what you built leads to pivoting the wrong variable.
- **Lean MVP spec:** buildable in *weeks, not months*. A landing page plus a spreadsheet is valid. *Extremely limited functionality*: a small initial user set and their highest-order problem. Ignore the rest for now. Vision for everyone, MVP for a few.
- **Historical MVPs:**
  - **Airbnb (2008):** no payments (cash exchanged in person), no map view, and the coder (Nate) was part-time.
  - **Justin.tv → Twitch:** one channel (Justin's life), barely recognisable low-res video, chat, no video games.
  - **Stripe as "/dev/payments":** no bank deals, few features. Founders *came to your office and integrated it themselves*, out of desperation for users and to find bugs before users did. A classic do-things-that-don't-scale tactic.
  - All became billion-dollar companies from "pretty bad" starts.
- **Heavy MVP exceptions:** regulated industries (insurance, banking, sometimes drones), hard tech (rockets), biotech, moonshots (tunnels). Even then, start with a simple *website explaining what you do* (buildable in days) so people you talk to have something to refer back to.
- **Launch misconception.** Nobody remembers the launch day of Google, Facebook or Twitter. Separate the **"any customers" launch** (do it ASAP) from the **press launch** (postpone). You can't learn whether your solution solves the problem until people use it. All the research and pitch-deck time is worth less than putting anything in front of a customer.
- **Speed hacks (the checklist):**
  1. **Time-box the spec.** Pick a date (e.g. 3 weeks). Only what fits by then goes on the spec.
  2. **Write the spec down.** Unwritten specs drift silently when a user or investor says "I'd never use that". A 3-week plan becomes 3 months. Writing it down makes changes visible and honest.
  3. **Cut the spec.** A week in you'll be over scope. Cut the unimportant, then cut important things if needed. Shipping anything creates momentum. Not shipping makes delay easy.
  4. **Don't fall in love with the MVP.** None of the examples ended up as their initial vision. It's like a first-grade essay.
- **Failure modes:** never getting a single user to touch the product. Dismissing feedback because "it's not the full thing". Changing the spec silently. Polishing a pitch deck instead of the product. Planning a big press launch.
- **Relevance to Sam.**
  - *Tereka*: he is his own user (Nigerian salary earner). An MVP could be a WhatsApp flow, a Google Sheet template, or a one-page web app that splits a payday amount into cups, built in a 2–3 week time-box. Payments, bank linking and multi-country support are the "Airbnb with no payments" omissions.
  - *Drive Button*: integrate it by hand for the first few bloggers (the Stripe move), to find bugs and learn.
  - *Riselist*: the landing page plus spreadsheet *is* the MVP. Keep the creator/brand problem fixed and change delivery.
  - With two jobs, a *written*, time-boxed spec stops evening-only projects drifting from weeks to months.

## Michael Seibel — The Biggest Mistakes First-Time Founders Make (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=D56QeyyQMLI)
**Most failures are self-inflicted: no real connection to the problem or users, fragile co-founder relationships, not launching, not measuring, and doing "sizzle" instead of "steak".**

- **1. Solving a problem you don't care about.** Not fatal (some founders learn to love it), but many startups die from *lost motivation*. The founder picked something that seemed wanted, fast-growing or cool rather than something worth 5+ years of their life.
- **2. Helping users you don't care about.** Justin.tv liked the abstract idea of democratising live video but didn't love the users who showed up. When Emmett (a gamer) refocused on gamers, the team regained love for its users, a core driver of Twitch's success. *Loving the user* is fuel for the years of iteration.
- **3. Co-founders you don't know well.** Exceptions exist, but have prior context (friend, co-worker, school project partner) so you know if you can get through hard times together.
- **4. No transparent co-founder conversations.** The recurring drama topics are **performance** (is my co-founder working as hard?), **goals** (same destination?) and **roles** (who owns engineering, product, customers?). Unspoken resentment builds and erupts later as fights. Hold regular, organised check-ins meant to share how you feel, not to argue.
- **5. Not launching.** Founders confuse launch with press exposure and fear it. Users don't remember the launch days of Snapchat, Instagram, WhatsApp, Uber or Lyft. Launch is far more significant to you than to them. You can't validate without users. For most consumer and B2B startups an MVP can be built and launched in **under a month**. Exception: heavily regulated markets (banking, lending) needing approval first.
- **6. No analytics.** Instrument what users actually do so you know what's used and what isn't.
- **7. No idea where first users come from.** The first 1–5 users should be people you already know or have identified, including yourself. "First 100 or 1,000" is a separate, harder question. If you know nobody with the problem, question the choice.
- **8. Sizzle over steak.** Prioritising press, hiring, conferences and investors over shipping product and talking to users is "cargo-culting" a startup. The real work is getting product into users' hands, seeing if they like it, and iterating.
- **Caveat he gives:** some startups made every mistake and still won. They are exceptions. Minimise these to improve your odds.
- **Relevance to Sam.**
  - Check motivation honestly across Tereka, Riselist, Trameter side-ideas, Photo Reunion and the LLM project: which problem and users would he serve for 5+ years? Spreading thin across many is its own version of mistake 1.
  - Tereka's users are people he knows (himself, sisters, friends, Starting Out community), so first users are not a mystery.
  - Riselist: love for TikTok creators and small brands should be real, or fix the segment.
  - Add analytics (PostHog/Plausible-level) from day one of any MVP.
  - Grants, conferences and branding are "sizzle" unless they directly unblock shipping or users.
