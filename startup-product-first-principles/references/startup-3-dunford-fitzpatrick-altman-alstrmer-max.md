# startup-3 — cross-cutting principles

1. **Ask about past facts in their life, never opinions of your idea.** Compliments are the "fake phone number" of feedback. Ask "last time this happened, what did you do?" and keep the idea out of it until the end, or entirely. (Fitzpatrick; Alströmer, Talk to Users)
2. **Users have good problems and bad solutions.** Feature requests point to an underlying problem: "inbox + email on one screen" meant Gmail was slow; "host's phone number" meant Airbnb lacked trust. Prioritising is your job, because users say yes to every feature. (Alströmer, Talk to Users)
3. **Behaviour beats words; payment beats praise.** Watch usage and cohorts, or ask for money. People can't predict their own future behaviour or what they'd pay. Price from what the status quo costs them today, then keep doubling until they push back. (Fitzpatrick; Alströmer, First Customers; Altman)
4. **The status quo is your biggest competitor.** Spreadsheets, pen and paper, interns, "do nothing": about 40% of B2B deals are lost to "no decision". To move someone off Excel you must be dramatically better. (Dunford; Alströmer, Talk to Users)
5. **Positioning is derived in order: alternatives → differentiated capabilities → value ("so what?") → best-fit customers → market category → sales story.** "Better" only means something as "what value, for whom". (Dunford)
6. **Keep positioning loose until a pattern appears, then tighten hard.** The tuna-net that turns out to be a grouper net. Tighten once diverse customers share a common thread and you can predict which pitches win. (Dunford)
7. **A small group who love it beats many who like it.** Love expands; lukewarm liking doesn't. Organic word of mouth is the signal; "a partnership will save us" is a warning sign. Most startups die from not making something loved, not from competitors. (Altman)
8. **Start simple, with less surface area than feels minimal, and be fanatical about details and support.** Early Google: a textbox, two buttons, the best results. Brex V1: a virtual card. (Altman; Alströmer, First Customers)
9. **Order of stages: learn → confirm → grow.** Selling before learning, and scaling before love, is the #1 killer. Premature scale and raising too early make mistakes fatal. (Fitzpatrick; Altman)
10. **Survival depends on the cost of mistakes, not their size.** Stay small and cheap ("cockroach mode") so a failed launch is a blunder, not death. Customer discovery runs on calendar time, so start it while still employed. (Fitzpatrick)
11. **Pick the company type that matches your goal and resources.** Scalable (investor-funded, years of losses), reliable (paid within about a month, profit-funded, no investors or house-backed loans) or freedom/lifestyle. Choose ideas within reach of your existing skills and network. (Fitzpatrick; Moskovitz)
12. **Market growth rate > market size; have a "why now?"; win a small market, then expand.** You can change everything except the market. Best ideas sound bad but are right, and fit in one sentence. (Altman)
13. **Founders sell and support personally; no one between founder and user.** Don't hire sales until you can sell; put your phone number on the site; instrument the founder→user feedback loop and improve 10% a week. (Altman; Alströmer, both talks)
14. **Sales is a numbers funnel; work backwards from the goal.** 500 sends → 250 opens → about 20 replies → 10 demos → 2 customers. 100 sends tells you nothing. Track each stage in a CRM, and always include onboarding as a stage. (Alströmer, First Customers)
15. **Choose the channel by the buyer's day.** Offline buyers are found at trade shows, by phone, at dinners, not by cold email. 3 days at a show beat 3 months of email for one founder. (Max)
16. **Customers 1–3 come from your network; 4–10 from unscalable work; tools only from about 10–50.** Warm intros, in-person visits, Reddit or community DMs, 6–10 person dinners, then Apollo and Clay once the message is worth scaling. (Max; Alströmer)
17. **Outreach copy: short (under 75 words, or 6–8 sentences), plain text, founder voice, social proof shown not told, one clear call to action, give value before asking, follow up 3–4 times.** Read it aloud to catch AI-sounding lines. (Max; Alströmer, First Customers)
18. **Charge from day one in B2B.** No free trials or unpaid pilots; use money-back guarantees or monthly opt-outs. If someone won't pay at qualification, move on. Easiest first customers: people you know, startups, early adopters. (Alströmer, First Customers)
19. **In B2B, positioning must win the champion.** Of the 5–7 people in a decision, only the champion matters at first: give them the story to justify the choice to their boss, IT and procurement. Segment by actionable company traits, not personas. (Dunford)
20. **Metrics shape the company: measure actives, retention, revenue and NPS, never total sign-ups.** Be brutally honest when they're flat. (Altman)

## April Dunford — How to nail your product positioning (Lenny's Podcast) (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hdjlCLb9Hl8)
**Positioning = how your product is the best in the world at delivering some value that a well-defined set of customers cares a lot about; derive it from the alternatives you must beat, not from internal opinions.**

- **Mental models / first principles**
  - Positioning is upstream of everything in marketing and sales. Messaging (homepage copy), branding and sales pitches are downstream outputs; you cannot write a message until you know who it is for and what value you deliver *versus what*.
  - If a product is underperforming, the product may be fine and the positioning wrong. Dunford's own career started by repositioning a "loser" product that then took off.
  - Positioning has five parts: (1) competitive alternatives, (2) differentiated capabilities, (3) differentiated value, (4) best-fit customers, (5) market category. Order matters: start with alternatives, end with category.
  - "Better" is meaningless on its own. Better has two halves: *what value* and *for whom*. Simpler-with-fewer-features is "better" for a small company; full-featured is "better" for an enterprise.
  - Great positioning feels obvious after the fact ("of course that's what it is, of course we need one"). Postman's "platform for building and using APIs" sounds trivial but took years to reach.
  - Positioning is not static: product and market both drift, so good positioning can decay. Periodically re-run the exercise.

- **Mechanisms (the process)**
  1. **Competitive alternatives** — what you must beat to win a deal. Two sets: the *status quo* (spreadsheet, pen and paper, interns, email) and the *short list* of other vendors. In B2B roughly 40% of deals are lost to "no decision", i.e. to the status quo. Ignoring it is a common mistake.
  2. **Differentiated capabilities** — list everything you have that alternatives lack: features, but also company capabilities (pricing, professional services, support model).
  3. **Value** — for each capability ask "so what?" for the customer. These cluster into 2–3 value themes. Doing it this way guarantees the themes are *differentiated*, not generic goods any alternative also provides. Asking the team "why do people love us?" yields untestable opinions instead.
  4. **Best-fit customers** — what characteristics make an account care *a lot* about that value? Anyone with the problem could buy, but not everyone values your differentiator equally.
  5. **Market category** — the context you place the product in so that your value is obvious to those customers. Starting from the category and working backward is "crazy" because you have no way to judge whether a category is good.
  6. **Turn it into a sales narrative** — storyboard, then deck/demo/script. Worked example, Help Scout (customer support software vs Zendesk, email, shared inboxes): capabilities = shared inbox built for service, you get a person not a ticket number, chatbot appears only when a real human is available; value = service that deepens the customer relationship; best-fit = D2C/e-commerce brands for whom support is their main customer touchpoint and a growth lever (not a phone company trying to minimise call-centre cost). Story: "support is a growth driver; alternatives treat your customers as numbers and push them to cheap channels".

- **Decisions & trade-offs**
  - **Early stage (pre-launch / handful of customers): hold a positioning *thesis*, keep external positioning loose.** Tuna-net analogy: you built a tuna net, but market it for "big fish" and see what fishermen catch; it might turn out to be great for grouper. Over-tightening too early means if the guess is wrong, you fail hard.
  - **When to tighten:** once you see a pattern, i.e. customers who look different share a thread (same tool, same team size), or you can predict which pitches will go well. Before that you only hear "all kinds of people like us for all kinds of reasons".
  - **DIY vs expert:** most companies can do it themselves with a method in a couple of days of locked-room work; outsiders help when opinionated executives can't agree or the stakes are high (about to hire a big sales team).
  - **Segments vs personas (B2B):** segment by actionable company traits you can build a list from (firmographics like size, revenue, geography, plus things like "creative team >3 people using tool X"). Personas describe people. Of the 5–7 people in a B2B decision, only the **champion** (the one asked to find a solution and build consensus) really matters for positioning; arm the champion to sell IT, legal, purchasing and the economic buyer. Making persona sheets for everyone is wasted effort.

- **Rules of thumb / checklist**
  - Run positioning as a team sport: CEO, sales, marketing, product, customer success in one room. Done only when everyone agrees.
  - Test positioning on *qualified prospects*, not friends, grandmothers or VCs. The real test: does a qualified prospect get excited and want to buy after hearing the story?
  - Help the buyer justify the choice to their boss: explain how the market splits ("these are for enterprises, these for X; if you are this, you need these four things, and we have them"). Otherwise they stall and stay on the spreadsheet.
  - Write positioning down as a document, then produce a sales narrative; positioning that stays a doc gets forgotten.

- **Failure modes / signals of weak positioning**
  - No single metric; weak positioning degrades the whole funnel: low marketing response at the top, sluggish mid-pipeline (understanding only clicks on the third sales call), and post-sale churn when customers realise the product isn't what they thought.
  - Sales-call tells: "can you back up and explain that again?"; "oh, so you're like Salesforce" (wrong mental category); worst: "I get what it does but why would anyone pay; I can do that in a spreadsheet".
  - Misalignment is the most common cause: founder, marketing, sales and product each tell a slightly different story. Two variants: founder pitches it perfectly but new hires can't; or the market shifted and the founder is still positioned for the old market.
  - Tech-first "gee whiz" launches without a "so what": Magic Leap and Google Glass later found B2B niches (manufacturing). Segway refused to say what it was ("revolution in human transport"), so users couldn't tell whether to compare it to a car or bike; it was unwelcome on both road and sidewalk.

- **Relevance to Sam**
  - **Tereka:** the main alternative is the status quo — mental accounting, a notebook, a WhatsApp note, a spreadsheet, or "ajo"/esusu savings habits — not other apps. Position against what Nigerians actually do now. Ask "so what?" for each feature (cups, allocations) until you reach a value theme, and find who cares *most* (e.g. salaried workers paid monthly, people with irregular income).
  - **Riselist:** in B2B the champion is the brand's marketing person who must justify the spend upward; give them a story about how the creator-marketing market splits and why a service-first agency fits brands like theirs.
  - **Trameter / Drive Button:** for B2B pitches to bloggers or partners, the status quo ("plain affiliate links" or "do nothing") is the alternative to beat. Keep early positioning loose and watch which kind of publisher pulls hardest before narrowing.
  - Solo founder with limited time: a two-day self-run positioning exercise is enough; no consultant needed.

## Rob Fitzpatrick — Start-up advice & How to talk to customers (The Mom Test), Digital Product School (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=FG1Fa-t4AEQ)
**Pick the type of company that fits your actual goal and resources, keep mistakes cheap by staying small, and learn from customers by asking about their lives (facts, past behaviour), never their opinion of your idea.**

- **Mental models / first principles**
  - **Three kinds of company, by founder motive** — each has a different revenue curve, lifestyle and funding fit:
    1. *Scalable* (huge impact or money): lose money for a long time while you find the "blueprint", then growth can go almost without limit. Needs investors; team stays tiny for years, then jumps (0 to 50 people). Lifestyle is miserable *once it works*. Payoff comes from selling the company (IPO or acquisition). Fitzpatrick compares it to Russian roulette: someone wins, but that doesn't make it sound career advice.
    2. *Reliable* (quit your job, support your family, low risk): the main test is **someone pays you quickly**, within about a month. Fund it from early profit and pay yourself dividends. Investors are "toxic" here; avoid bank loans secured on your house. Example: a restaurateur who opened one solid restaurant, took four years off, opened another. Fitzpatrick's last company: four founders, profitable, shut down when goals diverged, each paid a couple of years' salary.
    3. *Freedom / lifestyle* (travel, art, craft): niche, small money, but you live the life now. Example: a hot-sauce subscription (then grilled-cheese mail order) run with freelancers that let the founder retire to Portugal, a cheap country. Trap: opening a cafe "for freedom" gives meaning, not freedom.
  - Freelancing is the fastest model to validate (call old contacts this week, paid immediately) but caps out on your time.
  - **Modern startup theory in one sentence:** instead of planning everything and making one big jump, take small steps, gather new information, decide again. MVPs, landing pages, A/B tests and interviews are all tools for getting information sooner, so you make many small decisions instead of one big one.
  - **Cost of mistakes, not size of mistakes, decides survival.** Students in a living room with no expenses can launch to zero visitors and try again ("cockroaches... they just can't be killed"). Late-30s founders with a mortgage, fancy office and PR firm die from the same near-miss. Acting big before you're ready makes you fragile.
  - Pets.com burned about $300M discovering that pet food is not books. A website, and finding out nobody likes it, should be far cheaper than that.

- **Mechanisms: three stages, done in order**
  1. **Learn / discover** — Is it a real problem? Does a market and a budget exist? Is it legal? Can they fit it into their workflow? Answer by one-on-one conversations about their life, not market research. Don't pitch. Ask: "how do you deal with this now?", "what happened last time it went wrong?"
  2. **Confirm** — you have a "gift" (product). Now either ask for money, or ask for nothing and watch behaviour (analytics: daily use vs opened once). Burger-shop rule: you don't survey cheeseburger vs hamburger preference, you watch what sells. Behaviour doesn't lie; words do.
  3. **Grow** — only after a few people love it, can't live without it, pay and use it daily. Growth is where you can easily spend $1M a month on ads and sales. Pouring traffic into something nobody loves just wastes money.
  - **The #1 killer of scalable startups is doing steps out of order**: selling before learning, growing before a small group loves it.
  - Raising money too early is harmful: investors push for growth before the blueprint exists. Raise when you've proven it and can spend with confidence.

- **The Mom Test mechanics**
  - Pitching makes people want to escape politely: they give a compliment plus a stall ("so innovative, let me know when it launches"). Compliments are the "fake phone number" of feedback: they feel like progress but aren't.
  - Ask about facts in their life, especially the last time the problem happened. If you never mention your idea, they can safely say "honestly I just ignored it, it's not a big deal".
  - Worked example: Fitzpatrick's deal-flow tool for investors. Over a casual catch-up he complained about his own email, asked "how do you deal with it?" The investor got thousands of pitches but interns deleted most; he only saw 10–20 a week and spent a couple of hours on them. Four questions killed the idea for free.
  - Keep conversations casual. Formality adds bias: focus-group members try to sound smart; surveys and 1–5 scales give pie charts but lose depth. Talk like a friend hearing about a breakup ("that sucks, tell me about it"), not "on a scale of 1–5 how upset are you?".
  - You don't even need an idea: pick someone you want to serve (e.g. the elderly, or professionals with ageing parents), ask about the worst parts of their life and what they've tried. Ideas follow.
  - Note-taking: write exact quotes and emotional signals ("said X and was furious"), what they've tried, whether they spend money on it; replay the conversation play-by-play to the team. Small but honest data beats large biased data.

- **Decisions & trade-offs**
  - Choose ideas you can afford with *your* skills and assets. A finance person building an app pays for programmers; a programmer entering banking spends months getting meetings. Fitzpatrick lost two years in advertising with no contacts ("bought a horrific tan suit"). Play to your strengths now; harder ideas can come later when you have more resources.
  - Entrepreneurship is a career of several companies, each leaving you more skilled, connected and solvent, like an internship-to-CEO path.
  - Customer learning runs on calendar time, not clock time: meetings take weeks to arrange but hours to hold. Being full-time doesn't speed it up, so start while still employed (lunch breaks, evenings, a few days off).
  - Fitzpatrick has "tested about 50" ideas because exploring costs nothing; only a handful became companies.

- **Rules of thumb**
  - **How many conversations:** "six people" in qualitative testing. If your guess is right, six will confirm it consistently. If you get three clusters of two, you probably have several segments mixed together; narrow to one segment and do six again. Domain experience means better first guesses, so fewer iterations. Diminishing returns: stop when you keep hearing the same things.
  - **Make learning cheap in time:** add a few non-biased questions before the sales pitch (zero extra customer time); throw a party for your most active users (Songkick-style); during support tickets ask "why does this matter, what do you do without it?"; carry your key questions and ask them when you meet someone from the segment.
  - **Pricing:** people are terrible at predicting future behaviour and doubly bad at predicting what they'd pay. Better: ask how they handle it today and what that costs. Example: planned $200/month; the customer had six full-time staff doing it, roughly a $1M/year problem. Otherwise just sell it. Keep doubling the price until people stop paying, then undo the last doubling; you usually get two or three doublings. Grandfather early users at their price.
  - Use small-scale evidence to persuade investors ("2,000 users, but they check it every day") instead of "trust me".
  - Sketches on paper are fine; you're trying to learn, not impress a board.
  - When a big irreversible leap is unavoidable (a 10,000-unit run, a year of mass-market marketing), de-risk everything before it, then jump knowingly. Answer the easy questions first.

- **Failure modes**
  - Picking an idea because it looks impressive or like what Steve Jobs did, instead of from your goals. This was Fitzpatrick's biggest early mistake.
  - The emotional curve: launch hype ("TechCrunch of initiation"), "trough of sorrow" as novelty wears off and retention is zero, "wiggles of false hope", then either success or running out of money.
  - Fundraising overhead: months of legal email at $450/hour, artificial milestones, personal poverty (he had $1.37 days before a $1M wire; then the bank froze his account for money-laundering suspicion).
  - Glamorising hard things.

- **Relevance to Sam**
  - Sam is the textbook "reliable / bootstrapped, keep the day job" founder: solo in Lagos, limited capital, two jobs. Fitzpatrick's framework says choose ideas that pay within about a month or cost almost nothing to run, and do discovery on lunch breaks.
  - **Riselist** (service-first agency) is a "freelancing → small business" curve: quick to validate, paid fast, capped by time unless he trains people. Good as a cash engine.
  - **Tereka** is free, so the "confirm" signal is behaviour: daily or weekly active use, not compliments. Mom Test questions for Nigerians: "walk me through what happened to your last salary", "when did you last run out before month-end, and what did you do?", "what have you tried: apps, ajo, notebooks?" Never open with "would you use a money planner?".
  - **Trameter features:** for travel bookings, ask "tell me about the last trip you booked, where did you start?" rather than "would you click a booking button?".
  - Playing to strengths: Sam's edge is data, AI and pipelines, so ideas that use them are cheaper for him than ones needing sales networks he doesn't have.

## Sam Altman & Dustin Moskovitz — Lecture 1: How to Start a Startup (YC / Stanford CS183B) (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CBYhVcO4WgI)
**Success ≈ idea × product × team × execution × luck; of the parts you control, first get a good idea in a growing market, then build something a small number of users love, and only start if you can't *not* do it.**

- **Framing / scope**
  - All advice targets hyper-growth startups; much of it does not apply to small businesses or big companies (Altman warns about this explicitly).
  - Outcome is roughly idea × product × execution × team × luck, with luck "a random number between 0 and 10,000". Multiplicative: a zero anywhere kills it. Being young, poor and unknown can be an asset.
  - Start because a specific problem compels you and a company is the best way to solve it; the passion comes first, the startup second. Every big YC success followed this.

- **The idea (Altman)**
  - The "ideas don't matter, just pivot" pendulum has swung too far. Execution is about 10x more important and 100x harder than the idea, but great execution on a bad idea goes nowhere. Pivots rarely become big companies; the successful ones usually pivot into something the founders themselves wanted (Airbnb: Chesky couldn't pay rent but had spare space).
  - "Idea" is broad: market size and growth, growth strategy, defensibility. You'll work on it about 10 years, so spend real time up front. Plans are worthless, but planning is valuable and rare.
  - **Mission-oriented companies win**: teams work harder, you won't quit through years of pain, outsiders help more. Counter-intuitively it's often *easier* to start a hard startup than an easy, derivative one. Don't plan to do "the startup for 2–3 years then my passion": good startups take about 10 years.
  - Choosing between ideas: pick the one you think about most when you're not trying to think about work.
  - **Best ideas look bad at first** (13th search engine; 10th social network for broke students; sleeping on strangers' couches). If they looked good, too many people would be on them. So telling people your idea is usually safe.
  - **Monopoly path (Thiel):** you can't monopolise a big market immediately. Dominate a small, specific market, then expand. Good line: "today only a small subset will use it, but I'll get all of them; later almost everyone will."
  - Need conviction to ignore naysayers, on a fine line between right and crazy. Aim for "unpopular but right": sounds crazy, is actually right, few others working on it. V1 doesn't need to sound big.
  - **Market growth rate > current size.** Prefer a small fast-growing market over a big slow one; ask whether it will top out. Customers in fast-growing markets are desperate and tolerate imperfect but rapidly improving products. You cannot create a market that doesn't want to exist; you can change everything in a startup except the market. Tailwind framings: surf a wave, up-elevator, movement.
  - **"Why now?" (Sequoia):** why couldn't this be done two years ago, and why will two years from now be too late? Be suspicious if you lack an answer.
  - Best if you need it yourself. If not, get extremely close to customers: work in their office, talk to them multiple times a day.
  - **Explainable in one sentence.** Either very different from existing companies in one important way (Google = just search that works) or totally new (SpaceX). Clones with a made-up differentiator ("X but beautiful", "Y for red-wine lovers") usually fail.
  - Think market-first, about what customers want. This is the most common flaw YC sees in applications.

- **The product (Altman)**
  - "Product" includes support and the copy that explains it: everything in the customer interaction.
  - Until the product is great almost nothing else matters; fundraising, press, hiring and BD all get easier after. Early founders spend their time coding and talking to users. YC's instruction: build product, talk to users, exercise, eat, sleep; ignore PR, conferences, advisers, partnerships.
  - **Love vs like:** with a fixed "area under the curve", choose a small number of users who *love* it over many who *like* it. Expanding from love to more love is far easier than turning mass lukewarm liking into love. Medium-wanted products fail in confusing ways. "If you get this right you can get a lot of other things wrong."
  - **Signal: organic word-of-mouth growth**, consumer and enterprise alike. If you're explaining away lack of growth with "a big partnership will save us", that's real trouble. Building a growth machine before something is loved wastes time. Very few startups die from competition; most die from not making something users love.
  - **Start simple**, with even less surface area than you think is the minimum. Examples: early Facebook was almost comically simple; Google was an ugly page with a textbox and two buttons but the best results; iPhone was simpler than previous smartphones. Simplicity forces doing one thing extremely well.
  - **Fanaticism** about details, copy and support. Correlates with success at YC: founders who wired PagerDuty to the support inbox so night-time emails got an answer within an hour.
  - **Recruit early users by hand**, not via ads. Ben Silbermann recruited strangers in Palo Alto coffee shops for Pinterest and set Apple Store browsers to Pinterest. You need few users, but ones who give feedback daily.
  - **Feedback engine:** user feedback → product decisions → back in front of users → repeat. Ask what they like and dislike; watch them use it; ask what they pay for; "would you be really bummed if we went away?"; what would make them recommend it, and have they already? 10% better per week compounds fast; software loops can cycle in hours.
  - Founders do sales and support themselves early. Don't hire salespeople or support staff right away (a common Stanford-startup mistake).
  - **Metrics: the company builds whatever the CEO measures.** Ban talk of total registrations. Track active-user growth, activity levels, cohort retention, revenue, NPS, and be brutally honest when they're flat.

- **Why (or why not) to start — Moskovitz**
  - Four common but bad reasons:
    1. **Glamour.** Reality is heads-down desk work (support, sales, hard engineering), founder depression, fear of failing everyone who followed you, always on call, fundraising stress, unwanted press. You're committed: about 10 years if it works, about 5 if it doesn't (3 to discover it isn't working, 2 at the acquirer). Moskovitz was unhealthy and threw his back out every six months at 21–22. Managing your own psychology is a core CEO job (Horowitz).
    2. **Being the boss.** Your days are spent on other people's conflicts, "who do I disappoint least?". A key employee threatening to quit overrides Monday's grand plans.
    3. **Flexibility.** You're a role model; if you ease off, so does the team. Four-day weeks are only realistic for a deliberately small or niche business; past 2–3 people you must be fully committed.
    4. **More money and impact than joining a company.** Benchmark: employee #100 at about 10 basis points at Dropbox ≈ $10M; early Facebook ≈ $200M; even #1,000 in 2009 ≈ $20M. A founder typically ends at about 10% after dilution, so a $100M outcome ≈ $10M, and you must be *more* confident than in joining Facebook in 2009. Impact can also be higher inside big companies with distribution: Bret Taylor built Google Maps at about employee #1,500; Justin Rosenstein prototyped Gmail chat, then helped make the Like button at Facebook, which only worked because of Facebook's distribution.
  - **Best reason: you can't not do it**, in both senses: you're so passionate you'd do it anyway (needed to recruit; candidates smell its absence), and the world needs it *and* needs *you* specifically (if you're not the best-suited team, best case you beat the team who was, a worse outcome for the world). Asana began as a night-time internal tool at Facebook the founders couldn't stop building.

- **Failure modes**
  - Pivot-happy randomness; derivative clones; ignoring how the market will evolve (Altman calls investors' focus on current size a systemic error); hiring sales or support before founders do it themselves; vanity metrics; building growth machinery before love; starting a company for its own sake.

- **Relevance to Sam**
  - **Tereka:** "small market you can monopolise, then expand" fits Nigeria-first, then Africa, then global. Find the smallest group who'd *love* it (e.g. young salaried Lagos workers), not a broad "everyone who earns money". Why now: inflation and naira volatility plus smartphone ubiquity. Track weekly active planners and cohort retention, not sign-ups.
  - **Riselist:** service-first is consistent with "founders do sales and support themselves"; word-of-mouth referrals between brands is the organic-growth signal.
  - **Trameter / Drive Button:** a simple V1 with minimal surface area. Hand-recruit the first bloggers and use the five feedback questions.
  - **Two jobs:** Moskovitz's honesty matters. Hyper-growth paths eventually need full commitment past 2–3 people; until then Sam's constraint argues for ideas that can live as small, focused or niche businesses, or for proving love before quitting. Moskovitz's employee-equity maths is also a fair reminder that a great job with leverage can rival founding.
  - (note: the dollar figures are 2014 valuations; the reasoning holds but the numbers are dated.)

## Gustaf Alströmer (YC) — How to Get Your First Customers | Startup School (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hyYCn_kAngI)
**Startups don't take off by themselves; founders make them take off by manually selling. Sales is a numbers funnel you must instrument and work backwards from your goal.**

- **Mental models**
  - **Do things that don't scale** (Paul Graham's essay, built on Airbnb's early days). Good products are rarely built in isolation; they're built *with* customers, so the first version you show isn't that good yet. Founders avoid selling by "writing more code". That doesn't work.
  - **Startup curve** (Graham, labelled by Trevor Blackwell): launch spike (now Product Hunt or Hacker News, not TechCrunch), novelty wears off, *trough of sorrow* (no one has high retention at first; many die here by not testing new things fast enough), *crash of ineptitude*, *wiggles of false hope*, product-market fit. At every point it's the founders who make the difference: switching markets, learning to sell.
  - **Sales and talking to users are two sides of one coin.** If you don't understand the customer's problem you can neither build nor sell. You can't outsource sales any more than engineering; it must be in the founders' DNA. Don't hire a sales team until you can sell yourself; only then do you know what good looks like. And you can't tell whether the product is bad until you've genuinely tried selling it.
  - Sales is probably the easiest startup job to learn if you know the problem, product and market: the customer then sees you as the expert, and passion for their problem is infectious. Founder-sellers: Tony Xu (DoorDash), Mathilde Collin (Front), Tracy Young (PlanGrid), Steve Jobs.

- **Mechanisms**
  - **Brex example:** the first 10 customers came from their own YC batch. They asked "what is the minimum product useful to other startups?", which was just a virtual card; a founder personally onboarded every customer. Email hooks: "10 beta spots" (scarcity), "corporate card for tech companies" (speaks to the reader), "no personal guarantee" (differentiator vs alternatives), "free, merchants pay us" (no-brainer).
  - **Sales email rules:**
    1. Short: 6–8 sentences max (the Brex one was too long but worked).
    2. Clear language, no jargon.
    3. Address the recipient's problem.
    4. Plain text, no HTML, like writing to a friend.
    5. Say you're the founder; give social proof by showing, not telling (YC, past employers, known customers), not "I'm an expert with N years".
    6. Link to a simple site with product screenshots and bullets, no stock illustrations; or a short video or GIF that gets to the point instantly.
    7. One clear call to action (call, meeting or self-serve sign-up).
  - **Funnel, founder-speak vs sales-speak:** make the list (prospecting / lead gen) → contact (email or LinkedIn) → demo / meeting → pricing → close → **onboard**. Skipping onboarding causes churn because early products are hard to use. Keep a spreadsheet or simple CRM: industry, company, title, name, email, LinkedIn. Do the first batch by hand before outsourcing list-building, so you know exactly what to outsource.
  - **Work backwards from the goal (what most founders get wrong).** Example: 500 messages → 50% open (250) → about 5% reply (about 20) → 50% to demo (10) → 20% close = 2 customers. With 100 messages at the same rates you get about zero, and founders wrongly conclude "sales doesn't work, let's do SEO". The real answer: too few sends to even measure conversion. You cannot close 5 customers from 10 leads. Track every stage so mentors can diagnose whether you're bad at emails, demos or closing.

- **Decisions & trade-offs**
  - **First customers should be the easiest ones.** Build a big pipeline, then prioritise those most likely to close (read it from qualifying questions on the first call). Drop slow movers politely ("let's talk again in six months").
  - Selling to people you know > strangers. Selling to startups > big companies (short decision chains, no procurement department).
  - **Most people are not early adopters.** Silence isn't hatred; they just archive it. You need volume to *find* early adopters, not to convert sceptics. Gustaf was one at Airbnb, signing up for every founder's tool.
  - **Charge.** Not paying means not a customer, and no company. Payment proves value. Unwillingness to pay, discovered during qualification, is a signal to move on. B2B: no free trials or unpaid pilots; instead use a money-back guarantee (30/60 days) or a monthly option instead of an annual commitment. Consumer trials work best with card up front. Raise prices until customers complain but still pay.
  - **Early channels differ from scaled channels.** Airbnb's eventual growth (word of mouth, SEO, referrals, Facebook ads) is not how it got its first 2,000 users. SEM is expensive because of competition, SEO is slow, and ad-acquired users won't hop on a 30-minute call, so you can't learn from them. Find where your target users identify themselves online; for product-led or viral products, start through personal networks and co-workers. Enterprise sales at scale is just this process done by 100 people with more tooling.
  - Product-led doesn't mean big sales team or SEO; it means the product drives growth.

- **Rules of thumb**
  - Ask lots of questions early in the demo; founders run demos.
  - Proof that one email can work: examples led to a $72k/year contract, a specific landing of a named customer, and one template that won 22 customers.
  - Tools: Apollo.io, Close, Pipedrive, Hunter.io (contact finding). Reading: *Founding Sales*; Lenny's Newsletter (data on how B2B startups' first go-to-market looked: Amplitude, Stripe and Front started with founder outbound).

- **Failure modes**
  - Too little outreach because you never worked backwards from the target; believing something other than sales will solve sales; outsourcing sales; not qualifying on the first call; free pilots; jumping straight to "scalable" channels.

- **Relevance to Sam**
  - **Riselist** (B2B, brands): directly applicable. Build a list of Nigerian D2C and consumer brands already doing TikTok, send 300–500 short plain-text founder emails or DMs with creator-result screenshots, track open → reply → call → close in a sheet, and charge from day one (use a money-back or per-campaign guarantee instead of free pilots). Startups and small brands close faster than big FMCG with procurement.
  - **Trameter Drive Button:** treat bloggers as the "easiest first customers"; onboard each one personally.
  - **Tereka** (consumer, free): the "where do your users identify themselves online" rule points to Nigerian personal-finance Twitter/X, TikTok and WhatsApp communities, not paid ads, so you can still talk to early users. Being free means "charging" isn't the validation signal; retention and referrals must be.
  - Solo, two jobs, limited time: outbound is batchable (an evening a week for list-building and sending) and the CRM numbers tell you whether to keep going, rather than giving up after 30 sends.

## Gustaf Alströmer (YC) — How To Talk To Users | Startup School (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z1iF1c8w5Lg)
**Ideas come from two-way conversations with real users, not lone late-night insight. Interview about the problem, never the solution, then turn clustered notes into an MVP you test with the same people.**

- **Mental models**
  - The *Social Network* myth (idea from a lone coder) vs reality: Brian Chesky's early photo with Amal, Airbnb's first guest; they spent a weekend together. Great founders talk to users before having a product and keep doing it for the company's whole life.
  - Users and customers are the only stakeholders paying you, so they're the most likely to tell you the truth.
  - In 2010 Chesky gave up his apartment and lived in about 50 Airbnbs: the point was talking to a host every day. Brian and Joe still get host calls on their personal phones because they put their number on the site early. Test: search your inbox for "do-not-reply" to see how many companies avoid their customers.
  - **Users have good problems and bad solutions.** Gmail users asked for inbox and message on one screen; the real problem was that Gmail was slow. Airbnb guests asked for hosts' phone numbers; the real problem was insufficient trust in the platform.
  - Users have no incentive to say no to any feature. Prioritisation is your job.

- **Finding interviewees**
  - Personal network: most likely to respond, but may be less honest to avoid offending you. Still reach out.
  - Current and former co-workers: often know the domain; great for B2B software.
  - Outside your circle (common YC answers): LinkedIn, Reddit, forums, Slack or Discord communities, in-person events. Search LinkedIn for relevant title keywords (his mock carbon-emissions startup used "carbon", "climate", "sustainability").
  - Outreach message: introduce yourself, mention shared context, say you're starting a project (describe it only briefly), ask for a 20-minute call. Adjust the tone for strangers.
  - Define what you want to learn first. Mock example: does their company care about emissions, why or why not, and who inside cares most?

- **Running the interview**
  - Video, phone or in person: five minutes on video teaches more than 500 or 5,000 survey responses.
  - Build rapport; they may be answering questions nobody has asked them.
  - **Don't introduce your idea until the end, or at all**: it biases them. Listen; don't talk.
  - Use open follow-ups ("tell me about that", "what do you mean?", "why is that important to you?"). One answer rarely contains everything.
  - Take notes even if recording (you'll need notes anyway).
  - Mock dialogue: the CFO's company "cares", hires a consultant who produces a yearly PDF they don't act on; data is entered in a Word doc and seems inaccurate; regulation is coming. All of this surfaced without mentioning any product.
  - **Good questions:**
    1. Tell me how you do X today.
    2. What's the hardest thing about X?
    3. Why is it hard?
    4. How often do you do X?
    5. Why is X important to your company? (dig deep on motivation)
    6. What do you do to solve it today?
  - Best of all: watch them do it (screen-share the PDF, the spreadsheet). Behaviour change is the hard part, so understand current behaviour, not just words.
  - **Bad questions:** "Would you use our product?" (cheap yes); "Which features would make X better?" (that's your job); yes/no questions (you want concrete stories); "What would a better X look like?" (they aren't designers or PMs); two questions at once.
  - The big danger is drifting into features instead of problems.

- **From interviews to MVP**
  - After 5–10 interviews: notes for each, cluster on sticky notes or software into problem buckets, find the one that matters most, write conclusions, form a solution hypothesis. Don't over-intellectualise; design an MVP fast but on accurate information, and test it with the same users.
  - **Is the problem valuable?**
    1. Are people already paying for other solutions (e.g. consultants paid well for carbon PDFs)? That's a good sign.
    2. Are they happy with "basic" solutions like Excel or Google Sheets? Spreadsheets are a formidable competitor to hundreds of startups; you must be *dramatically* better to justify hundreds of dollars a month.
    3. How easy is the audience to sell to? Plumbers and contractors rarely change tools; startups try new things constantly.
  - **Prototype testing:** clickable mocks (Invision) shown to people in the lobby or coffee queue. Give a goal ("book a stay with these dates"), don't tell them how, and have them think aloud, revealing which words and screens they don't understand.
  - **Keep interviewees involved:** a Slack or WhatsApp group framed as exclusive early access; show progress often; ship a new screen in response to their comment to build trust in your responsiveness; let them meet peers with the same problem (they value that).

- **Relevance to Sam**
  - **Tereka:** a WhatsApp early-access group is ideal for Nigeria. Ask "tell me how you handle salary week", "what's the hardest part of making money last the month?", "what do you use today?" and get them to screen-share their notes app, spreadsheet or bank app. The spreadsheet / notebook / mental-maths status quo is the real competitor, so Tereka must be dramatically easier, not slightly.
  - Watch for "bad solutions" in feature requests: someone asking for bank-sync may really need less manual entry, or reassurance.
  - **Riselist:** interview brand marketers on LinkedIn: "how do you run TikTok campaigns today, what's hardest, what do you pay agencies or creators now?". Existing spend on influencers proves value.
  - **Trameter:** watch travellers try a Drive Button prototype with a goal ("book this tour from the article") and think aloud.
  - Sam can run 20-minute calls in lunch breaks; the six questions make each one efficient.

## Max (YC Visiting Partner) — How to Get Your First 10 Customers (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_FBivfgOvuE)
**Tactics crowd-sourced from dozens of YC founders: find where your buyer actually spends time; customers 1–3 come from your network, 4–10 from unscalable in-person and community work, and tools only matter from about 10–50.**

- **Mental models**
  - **Channel follows the buyer's day, not your convenience.** Cold email and LinkedIn are popular because they're laptop-friendly, automatable and *feel* like work. They suit buyers who live on computers (selling sales tools to sales leaders). They fail for school administrators, property managers, insurance agents and truck dispatchers. One founder spent months emailing a legacy industry with terrible open and reply rates, then closed more in 3 days at a trade show than in 3 months of email; subject-line tweaking was wasted effort.
  - **Early buyers buy the founder.** A bet on a new product from a new company requires trust in the person; your first- and second-degree networks trust you most.
  - **The founder's personal effort is the moat right now.** Showing up, DMing, a deeply researched email: it signals you care enough to spend your own time, which incumbents and automation can't fake.

- **Mechanisms / playbook**
  - **Step 0, one hour on the buyer's day:** What does their average day look like? How often do they check email? Which conferences do they attend? Are they on Reddit or LinkedIn? Do they answer the phone? Do they choose tools by asking friends? Which industry newsletters do they read? If you can't answer these concretely, you haven't spent enough time with customers; fix that first.
  - **Warm network, in order:** (1) personal: former colleagues, classmates, friends in the industry; (2) second-degree LinkedIn intros (one founder got about half her batch-time customers this way); (3) AI network search (e.g. Happenstance, natural-language search across your extended network). Make intros easy: who you want, why they'd care, and a forwardable blurb.
  - Prospecting tools only matter after about 10–20 quality customers. Many founders spend weeks setting up tools while leaving second-degree connections unmessaged.
  - **Show up in person.** One founder flew to the same executive four weeks running despite reschedules and closed. Another arrived uninvited at offices (usually asked to leave); one Hawaii visit ended after 8 minutes, yet that customer became a top account.
  - **Small, industry-specific conferences** convert far better than cold email. Playbook: a Calendly with back-to-back 15-minute slots across every conference day; email the attendee list a couple of times before and again during the event; stack meetings all day.
  - **Micro-events:** dinners or happy hours for 6–10 ideal-customer-profile people at about $50–100 a head. They convert better than big events; people who ate with you don't ignore your follow-up.
  - **Show up where the pain is expressed in public** (consumer / SMB): Reddit, Facebook groups, Discord, YouTube comments; for B2B niches, industry forums and trade-association boards. Patterns: a product video on Reddit gets torn apart in comments while hundreds of lurkers quietly sign up; one founder DMed every commenter in old complaint threads and got his first 10 customers; a healthcare founder replied to Reddit and Facebook complaints 2–5 times a day for months (some shadowbans, many customers). Reddit threads are indexed by Google and persist for years, so the effort compounds.
  - **Outbound tools (when you get there):** Apollo (lead database, email finding, simple sequencer; free tier enough to start), Clay (AI enrichment and qualification, e.g. what software they use, who's hiring, who posted about X), LinkedIn Premium (freshest data; send a connection request with no note, then a short DM after acceptance; one founder's biggest customer and best cold channel).
  - **Non-pitch framings (only if genuine, never a disguised sales call):**
    - Ask for mentorship: short real notes to dozens of CEOs; a few accepted, some became customers.
    - Research at scale: one founder talked to 200 salespeople before building, maxing weekly LinkedIn invites around one hypothesis per week; about 50% accepted, about 20% of those became calls; she had a pipeline by launch.
    - Free whiteboarding: a DevTools founder offered free agent-architecture sessions plus a shared Slack; the architecture needed his product.
    - Pay for feedback: a legal-tech founder paid lawyers $100–200/hour for product feedback; about 30% accepted and CAC stayed reasonable. Works in high contract-value markets.
  - **Copy rules:** under 75 words (long reads as ignorable or LLM-written); one clear call to action (reply? 15-minute demo?), otherwise they assume you'll eat their time; read it aloud to a friend and rewrite anything you wouldn't say to a person (a one-minute de-AI check). **Give before you ask:** a vulnerability scan of their public site, specific suggestions on their app's onboarding, or a short compliance audit note on their product. About 20 minutes of work for 30 minutes of their time is fair. Follow up 3–4 times over a couple of weeks.

- **Decisions & trade-offs**
  - **1–3 customers:** personal network (essentially no counter-examples in his survey). **4–10:** unscalable work (flights, Reddit DMs, small dinners, personalised DMs, free consulting); tedious, but you learn before automating. **10–50:** refined pitch, case studies, a clear value prop; now Apollo, Clay and sequences make sense because the message is worth scaling.
  - In-person is slower, awkward, costs money and risks face-to-face rejection, but works better than nearly anything else for the first 10.

- **Failure modes**
  - Defaulting to cold email for offline buyers, then concluding "outbound doesn't work"; polishing copy instead of changing channel; buying tools before working your network; long or AI-sounding emails with no clear ask; no follow-up; deceptive "advice" framing.

- **Relevance to Sam**
  - **Riselist:** many Nigerian SME brand owners live on WhatsApp and Instagram DMs and at physical markets and events, not email. Map the buyer's day first. Warm network first (former WHO/UNICEF agency contacts, Gombella and Trameter colleagues, Yabatech classmates). "Give before ask" fits perfectly: send a brand a 3-point teardown of its TikTok or a sample creator shortlist before requesting a call. Lagos micro-dinners or founder meetups (Starting Out events are a natural venue) are cheap at Lagos prices.
  - **Tereka:** pain is expressed publicly on Nigerian Twitter/X ("salary finished by the 10th"), Nairaland, TikTok comments and Facebook groups. Reply as a real person and DM complainers. Nairaland and Reddit threads persist in Google results for years.
  - **Trameter Drive Button:** travel bloggers complain in blogging Facebook groups and Reddit (r/Blogging, r/TravelBlogging). Offer a free audit of their monetisation (give before ask).
  - **Time-poor founder:** prioritise the highest-conversion, lowest-tool-cost channels (warm intros, community replies). Defer Apollo and Clay until there's a message worth scaling.
  - (note: Happenstance is presented as a recent YC company; check it still exists before relying on it.)
