# startup-2 — cross-cutting principles

1. **PMF is a ladder, not a switch.** Four levels (nascent 3–5 paying customers → developing 25 → strong ~100 → extreme), each optimising a different mix of satisfaction, demand, efficiency. Satisfaction first, then demand, then efficiency. (Todd Jackson)
2. **Efficiency is part of PMF.** Demand and delight bought by selling $2 for $1 (the "$100 vending machine") is not fit. The marginal customer should get steadily easier and cheaper to win and serve. (Todd Jackson; cf. Tom Blomfield on cost as a floor)
3. **Measure retention, not signups or press.** Track D1/D3/D7/D30 returning usage, NPS (>40–50), and cohort renewal rates; press spikes decay, so watch the baseline trend. Monthly retention of 95% vs 90% leaves 54 vs 28 of 100 customers after a year. (David Rusenko; Aaron Epstein)
4. **Listen to problems, not solutions; don't be a waiter.** The customer is the expert on the problem, the founder on the solution. Feature requests are orders, not insights. (Rusenko; Dalton & Michael)
5. **Hunt for surprise.** If you can predict everything users will say, you won't find anything new. The valuable clues are counterintuitive and best found by watching people work in their own environment. Use AI to reach baseline domain knowledge first so real visits go deeper. (Dalton & Michael) Balance: stop *validating* once you can predict 70–80% of answers. (Todd Jackson)
6. **Test money, not opinions.** Look for "wow" statements or demonstrated behaviour (follow-ups, asking for the deck to share internally); "interesting", "no budget", "next year" mean no. Probe existing budget, approval process, and fair/expensive/prohibitive price. (Todd Jackson)
7. **Pick the persona and problem carefully, then pivot with the four Ps.** Founders spend 99% of their time building and 1% picking. When stuck, change persona, problem, promise, or product. The cheap levers (promise/positioning, mock-ups) come before rebuilding. Most founders do 10% pivots when they need 200% ones. (Todd Jackson, citing Kopelman and Altman)
8. **Founder–market fit and unusual vantage points beat brainstorming.** ≥70% of top YC companies' ideas came organically. Mine your own jobs and life for problems you're unusually placed to see; pick fertile idea spaces so adjacent ideas exist if the first fails. (Jared Friedman)
9. **Avoid SISPs and tar pits; welcome schleps, boring spaces, and competitors.** Google the idea and learn why predecessors failed. No competitors often means no demand. A fresh spin on an existing, budgeted category (Ironclad as a "better CLM", Dropbox as the 20th storage company) is easier than inventing a category. (Friedman; Todd Jackson)
10. **Iterate cheaply and fast.** Expect ~10x more iterations than planned (~27 loops). Keep burn low; keep building in-house (outsourcing is one-shot); use prototypes, mock-ups, Wizard-of-Oz or manual delivery before full builds. Optimise for learning by attacking the biggest unknown first. (Rusenko; Todd Jackson on Vanta, Lattice)
11. **Ship a "minimum remarkable product".** Launch when it is better than alternatives at the one job that matters, not on a feature grid. Focus on customers, not competitors. Three silent UX tests catch the critical bugs. (Rusenko)
12. **Copy a proven business model; innovate on product.** SaaS, transactional, and marketplace models account for 67% of YC's top 100. Recurring revenue with high retention, as close to the transaction as possible, scaling with software. Services, affiliate, ads (unless you'll be a top-10 site), hardware, and platform-dependent businesses rarely become giants. (Aaron Epstein)
13. **Charge early, price on value, assume you're undercharging.** Pricing is a learning tool. Find the right order of magnitude first. The ideal price makes customers complain but still pay. Cheaper-than-X is not a moat, and a low price can signal low trust (Segment: $120/yr → $18K/yr). Pricing isn't permanent; keep it simple. (Aaron Epstein; Tom Blomfield)
14. **B2B value equation.** Write down quantified value with the champion (time saved, cost cut, revenue gained), price around 25–50% of it (customer keeps ~2/3), and use it as the pilot's success criteria. Cost is a floor (target 80–90% gross margin, count credits as cash). Differentiate rather than fight price wars. (Tom Blomfield)
15. **Price, channel, and model must fit.** A salesperson needs roughly 5x their compensation in new ARR. ~$75/month is a dead zone: too cheap for sales, too dear for impulse buys. Sales-led needs bigger tickets; cheap products need self-serve or organic channels. Slow sales cycle + low ACV = "quadrant of death". (Rusenko; Tom Blomfield; Todd Jackson)
16. **Prefer committed recurring revenue and short, decisive pilots.** Convert usage pricing to flat commitments; price pilots under the champion's signing authority; 2–4-week proofs of concept, or annual contracts with a 30–60-day money-back guarantee; no long free trials. (Tom Blomfield)
17. **Stay small and close before PMF; scale hard after.** Founders should know everything about customers. Don't pass ~20 people pre-PMF. After PMF, race for #1, but never more than double headcount a year. Be close enough to customers to have the "are we a necessity or am I friend-zoned?" talk. (Rusenko; Todd Jackson citing Rick Song)
18. **Know when to stop.** Even great founders rarely "feel" PMF, so rely on metrics. But 4–5 years without market pull rarely turns around, and returning money or a soft landing is no shame. (Todd Jackson)

## Todd Jackson (First Round Capital, on Lenny's Podcast) — A framework for finding product-market fit (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yc1Uwhfxacs)
**PMF is not binary: it is four levels (nascent, developing, strong, extreme) across three dimensions (satisfaction, demand, efficiency), and when stuck you change one of the four Ps (persona, problem, promise, product).**

- **Mental models / first principles**
  - Definition of "extreme PMF": widespread demand for a product that satisfies a critical need AND can be delivered repeatably and efficiently to each customer. Three dimensions: demand, satisfaction, efficiency. Most people drop efficiency.
  - "$100 vending machine" (Brett Berson): put in $1, get $100. Infinite demand, delighted users, great retention, zero viability. Selling $2 for $1 can look like PMF; it is not. WeWork and Casper had demand and satisfaction but never efficiency.
  - **Marginal customer**: the next customer should keep getting easier and cheaper to win and serve. If it is not, PMF is not strengthening.
  - The three dimensions trade off against each other: marketing spend buys demand but costs efficiency; heavy automation buys efficiency but can hurt satisfaction. What you optimise is different at each level.
  - Framework targets sales-led B2B. Consumer/bottom-up is closer to "catching lightning" (taste, timing); B2B sales-led has more science.
  - Josh Kopelman: founders spend 99% of time building and 1% picking market/problem/customer, yet the pick sets the boundaries for the next decade. Spend real time on the pick.

- **The four levels (rough benchmarks, wide error bars)**
  - **L1 Nascent**: pre-seed/seed, <10 people, goal 3–5 *paying* customers with an important AND urgent problem, delivered a satisfying solution. Priority: satisfaction > demand > efficiency. Inefficiency is fine (Vanta delivered a hand-filled spreadsheet; Ramp had people updating dashboards behind the scenes). Demand is mostly warm network; ~20 warm intros per customer, so ~50 conversations for 3–5. $0–500K ARR. Efficiency metrics (burn multiple, gross margin, NRR) are not applicable yet. Ideal duration 12–18 months, because the persona/problem choice is the most important decision.
  - **L2 Developing**: 5 → 25 satisfied customers. Grit gets you to 5–10; beyond that the product must do the heavy lifting. Add demand: early scalable channel (cold outreach, content, community events). Cold first-call-to-close ~10%. $500K–5M ARR. Magic number (new ARR / CAC in period) 0.5–0.75. Regretted churn 10–20% tolerable, NRR ≥100%. Guardrails: gross margin not below 50%, burn multiple (burn / net new ARR) not worse than 5x. 5→25 in about a year is a strong PMF signal.
  - **L3 Strong**: 25 → ~100 customers, 30–100 people, ~Series B, $5–25M ARR, at least one cracked scalable channel, ≥10% inbound from referrals/word of mouth, you are "known for a thing". Efficiency now matters: gross margin >60–70%, burn multiple 1–3, regretted churn <10%, NRR >110%. "Boulder rolls downhill"; Lattice founder: leads appear and you don't know where they come from.
  - **L4 Extreme**: >100 customers, >$25M ARR, first-call close >15%, magic number >1, CAC payback <12 months, gross margin >80%, burn multiple <1, NRR >120%. Growth now needs TAM expansion: new markets or multiple products (Vanta, Verkada, Stripe, Square), which means finding PMF again for each new product. Customer expectations keep rising (Casey Winters).
  - Whole journey ~4–6 years for good enterprise companies. 60–70% never get past L1/L2; ~30% reach L3/L4.

- **Stuck signals per level (yellow flags)**
  - L1 (6–12+ months): customers wouldn't be very disappointed if you vanished; each customer's most important feature is different (you're a consultancy, not a product); next customer is very hard; usage is low and flat; customers buy one quarter then quietly don't renew (Lattice's OKR tool).
  - L2 (12–18 months): current customers happy but you can't "open the floodgates"; regretted churn >20%; long sales cycles, late-funnel losses to competitors; price pushback. "No budget" / "not the right time, talk next year" means **no**. Worst quadrant: slow sales cycle + low ACV ("quadrant of death"). $20–30K ACV needs fast cycles; $100–200K ACV can tolerate 3–6 months.
  - L3: leaky bucket (NRR <90%, regretted churn >10%), growth slowing (3x → struggling for 2x), first channel saturating, competition arrives, growth only possible by pushing burn multiple back above 3.

- **The four Ps (pivot levers)**
  - Persona (think of the market as a countable set of people with money and problems, not an abstract category), Problem, Promise (positioning/what you pledge), Product. Product must deliver a promise that solves the persona's problem.
  - Lattice kept persona (HR heads), changed problem/promise/product from OKRs to performance management; sold first 5–10 customers on Figma mock-ups, timed with performance reviews coming back into fashion.
  - Vanta changed all four (from "B2B Alexa" meeting recorder and dropshipping to compliance). Plaid kept the product (bank-connection code from a failed consumer budgeting app) and flipped persona/problem/promise to fintech developers.
  - Ironclad changed only the promise: "AI legal assistant" (a new category nobody searched for) → "a better CLM" (existing category with existing budget). Rule: a fresh spin on an existing category is usually easier than inventing a category, because buyers already have budget and are already shopping.
  - Jack Altman: founders do 10% pivots when they need 200% pivots. Sunk cost makes this psychologically hard, especially once at $1–3M ARR.
  - Todd prefers sell-before-build: change persona/problem/promise first (cheap), product last (expensive).
  - Usual root cause when stuck: the problem is not burning enough, or the promise is not valuable enough.

- **Dollar-driven discovery (customer interviews that test money, not opinions)**
  - People are polite and bad at predicting their own behaviour, but good at describing their problems. Avoid "happy ears": hearing only what supports your idea.
  - Step 1, extreme value: ask about their goals for the next quarter, then "what's hard about those?" without mentioning your product. When you pitch a promise, you want a **wow statement** ("does that really work? I'd sign up today") or **demonstrated behaviour** (asks for a follow-up, asks for the deck to share internally). "Interesting" = polite no. Ask "what stands out as valuable?" and expect a fast, concrete answer (revenue, savings, risk).
  - Step 2, ability to pay: are you already looking for / building something for this? (Best customer: knows the problem, is shopping, or tried to build it and failed.) Where would budget come from (existing tool spend, engineers allocated)? How are third-party tools approved (spend thresholds, comparison rules)?
  - Step 3, willingness to pay: what do you pay for the alternative; would you pay more or less for mine? Van Westendorp-style triple (via Madhavan Ramanujam): fair price, expensive price, prohibitively expensive price. The "expensive" number is often what they will actually pay if the product is good.
  - Pick demo fidelity to match the product: Lattice sold on mock-ups; Looker needed customers' own data modelled (20–40 hours of pre-sale work, later formalised as "forward deployed"; led to 75% close and near-zero churn); Vanta/Pilot sold by just doing the work.
  - Enough interviews = you can predict 70–80% of what the next person will say.
  - Persona (Rick Song) "don't get friend-zoned": sit early customers down one-on-one: "don't be nice; are we a necessity? if we vanished how painful? if a competitor charged half, would you switch?"

- **Rules of thumb**
  - Early customers must pay. Wizard-of-Oz/manual delivery is fine at L1.
  - Founders who reach PMF are close to customers: texting them, meeting on weekends, friends with them.
  - After 4–5 years with no market pull, returning money or a soft landing is legitimate; late resurrections are rare.
  - Even successful founders rarely "feel" PMF (Retool felt every customer was the last until a few $M ARR; Databricks unsure at $100M). Healthy paranoia is good, but use metrics, not feelings.

- **Relevance to Sam**
  - Riselist (service-first agency) sits at L1 by design: manual, inefficient delivery is fine IF each client is deeply satisfied and paying. Watch the consultancy trap: if every client wants a different core deliverable, you have not found a product. Use the "friend-zone" talk with first brands.
  - Tereka is consumer/free: the satisfaction dimension can be measured early ("very disappointed if gone"), but efficiency (cost per user served, eventual revenue path) must be designed in; avoid a $100 vending machine that grows on subsidy.
  - Trameter features (Drive Button, booking flows): position in existing categories buyers already budget for (e.g. "affiliate booking widget for travel bloggers") rather than inventing a new category.
  - With two jobs and limited capital, spend disproportionate time on the pick (persona + problem) and on cheap levers (promise/positioning, mock-ups) before writing large amounts of code.

## David Rusenko (Weebly founder) — How To Find Product Market Fit (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0LNQxT9LvM0)
**PMF comes from many fast, cheap loops of "talk to customers about problems, prototype, test, measure retention"; expect ~10x more iterations than you think, so keep burn low and the team able to iterate in-house.**

- **Mental models / first principles**
  - Weebly's own curve: first line of code Feb 2006; record signup day of 12 users at 6 months; press spikes (TechCrunch, Newsweek, Time) each jumped then decayed, and the baseline trend between spikes was *declining*. No PMF at 18 months. The curve finally turned upward around 20 months (~1,000 signups/day), and clearly took off by year 4. Press spikes are not PMF; the trend between them is the signal.
  - Company stages: idea → prototype → launch → traction → monetization → growth. Getting from launch to traction is the PMF search and kills most companies. Monetization usually comes after traction and is an easier problem. You keep refining PMF during growth too.
  - Only two truly hard things in a startup: finding PMF and building a world-class team. Making money is a distant third: it is easier to monetise people who are already hooked.
  - Modified YC mantra: "make something a lot of people want" (folds in market size).
  - The best companies find a **hidden need**: if the need were obvious, everyone would do it, so expect people to call the idea dumb (Meetup's founder publicly called Weebly the stupidest idea he'd heard). Market research only describes existing markets, so it cannot find a new one.
  - Jobs-to-be-done: people do not want a website; they want to launch and grow a business. Ask what you substitute for (Facebook page, etc.).
  - New-market products often look like a weaker version of an existing category (iPhone had no 3G or apps; Weebly had less than Dreamweaver) but open the category to new entrants, then later cannibalise incumbents.
  - Watch for **pull**: when users hack your product to do something it wasn't built for, double down. You should not be pushing customers toward your solution.

- **The literal "step 3" loop**
  1. Talk to customers, form a market thesis: pain, job to be done, how to do it faster/better.
  2. Listen to their **problems, not their solutions** (proposed solutions are usually poor). Jobs ignored stated solutions but still talked to customers and iterated heavily.
  3. Rapid prototype + user test. Building a fully functional product to test a hypothesis is very expensive; aim for a functional prototype (clickable mocks, throwaway code).
  4. Build the solution. 5. Test it with them. 6. Did it work? Repeat. 7. Expect ~27 loops; nobody gets it right first time.
  - Implications: keep burn low (if you can afford 2–3 iterations but need 20–30, you die); build a team that iterates fast in-house. Outsourcing early code fails because outsourcing is one-shot.
  - Don't worry about scaling infrastructure early ("millions will show up on launch day" doesn't happen). Don't obsess over monetisation initially, but don't be afraid to charge.

- **Testing tools (only three needed)**
  - Customer interviews: 5–10, about an hour each, in their context; learn motivations and pain.
  - UX testing sessions: 3–5 (3 finds the critical bugs). Protocol: person uses the product in front of you; encourage honest feedback; give a task; do not touch the device; **say nothing** until they finish, even if they ask for help. Story: a signup form with only email + password was read as a login form; adding a pointless "name" field fixed it. People don't read.
  - Metrics: sample sizes are always too small; beware storytelling from A/B tests with ~17 users.
  - Talk to target customers, but be flexible: if unexpected users show up and do interesting things, maybe they are the real target. Don't overthink; anecdotal evidence is OK early.

- **When to launch / prioritise**
  - "Minimum remarkable product" rather than minimum viable: the least you can build that is remarkable. Paul Buchheit: launch when your product is better than what's out there.
  - "Better" is not a feature grid. Focus on customers over competitors (copying competitors means you're always 3 months behind; a well-funded rival cloned Weebly's UI just before Weebly changed it). Win on the one job or one aspect (often ease of use) people need most.
  - Only work on what gets you to the next stage. Skip conferences, blog posts, news.
  - Astro Teller: cost × benefit sorting is good for continuous improvement; for discontinuous improvement, **optimise for learning**: attack your biggest unknown first, because the answer rewrites the whole priority list.

- **PMF metrics**
  - Returning usage: of people who sign up, what % come back within 1, 3, 7, 30 days. Friends and family not coming back is a damning early signal.
  - NPS: % 9–10 minus % 0–6. Above ~40–50 suggests PMF. Early Weebly: 88% promoters − 8% detractors = 80. Can be negative early.
  - Paying-customer **renewal rate** (cohort-based: of those eligible to renew, % who did). He prefers it over simple churn (lost / total active), which is distorted when a fast-growing denominator hides losses.
  - Not useful: raw signups (track active users; with bad retention, actives can shrink while signups grow); early SaaS conversion rates (start low and build).
  - Weebly admitted they did not track the right KPIs during the 18 months, which is probably why it took so long.
  - Feel: without PMF everything is pushing a rock uphill and customers are too nice to say no but don't come back. With it, customers beat a path to your door.

- **Beyond PMF: product/market/channel/model fit**
  - Need: product meaningfully better than alternatives; a differentiated, scalable acquisition channel; a business model that doesn't kill traction.
  - Channel–model fit: ~$75/month is a dead zone: too low to pay for salespeople, too high for impulse credit-card purchase. Sales-led needs roughly ≥$200–300/month; self-serve should be ≲$25/month. (Reference: HubSpot growth framework.)
  - Pricing story: Weebly was free 2006–2008 (2007-era Valley thinking: revenue caps your valuation; note: outdated). Users mailed unsolicited $100 cheques. Launched Weebly Pro at $4/month in 2008; first week earned 10x less than the most pessimistic bet; kept iterating, cut founder salaries, cash-flow positive Jan 2009.

- **Team and fundraising**
  - Don't scale past ~20 people before PMF. At ~23–25 a flat team breaks and needs management layers, which are bad for PMF search. Pre-PMF, founders should know everything about customers, product, market, channels; micromanaging is acceptable; don't delegate anything important.
  - After PMF, scale aggressively: you are racing for a new market and advantages compound for the #1 player (more people → faster product → more revenue → more financing). But never more than double headcount per year.
  - Most companies cannot raise until early traction (big pre-launch rounds go to founders with prior hits). Bridge by sweat: a few people who can build everything themselves, no outsourcing, very low burn.
  - Brand: build it on a fundamental customer insight, ideally the same one the product rests on. Virgin America: can't win on schedule, price, destination, or loyalty programme, so win on "flying sucks": better experience for ~$15 more; every decision (lighting, staff pay, PR on planes) reinforces it.
  - Founders are not reckless risk-takers: they are optimists and determined. A company fails when you give up, but don't plod on blindly against all evidence.
  - Horizontal vs vertical: verticalising is usually good advice; Weebly went horizontal because no single vertical was deep enough to pay for the full required feature set (blogging, e-commerce, forms, CMS), then verticalised later.
  - Creating a market: the first 3–4 years were spent convincing people website-building was possible for them; the "big bang" comes when enough people try it and word spreads (Airbnb/Uber).

- **Relevance to Sam**
  - Tereka (consumer, free): track D1/D3/D7/D30 returning usage and NPS, not signups. Run 3-person silent UX tests with real Nigerian users on the first onboarding flow; a $0 tool for a solo builder. "Organising money" is the hidden need; the job is "not running out before month-end", not "use a planner".
  - Working solo with two jobs: optimise for learning; test the biggest unknown first (e.g. will users return weekly?) with throwaway code before building polished infrastructure. Keep burn near zero so you can afford 20–30 iterations.
  - Pricing/channel fit check for Riselist and Trameter B2B features: the price point must pay for how you acquire customers (founder-led sales needs higher-ticket deals; low-priced offers need self-serve/organic channels).
  - Don't chase press spikes for launches; look at the baseline trend afterwards.

## Jared Friedman (YC) — How to Get and Evaluate Startup Ideas | Startup School (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Th8JoIan4dg)
**Start from a real, acute problem your team is unusually placed to see; score it on ten questions; prefer schleppy, boring, competitive spaces; and if unsure, launch to find out.**

- **Sources of the advice**: analysis of how the top 100 YC companies (by valuation) got their ideas; Paul Graham's "How to Get Startup Ideas"; helping mid-batch pivots; patterns in thousands of rejected applications.

- **Four common mistakes**
  1. **Solution in search of a problem (SISP)**: "AI is cool, what can I apply it to?" You will find a superficially plausible, made-up problem people don't care about. Fall in love with a problem instead, but a specific, tractable one, not "global poverty".
  2. **Tar-pit ideas**: widespread, easy-looking problems that founders have pitched for ~20 years (e.g. the app to coordinate weekend plans with friends) with a hidden structural reason they are hard. Not impossible, but much harder than they look. Defence: Google the idea, find and talk to people who tried it, identify the hard part that beat them.
  3. Jumping into the first idea without asking whether it is a good business.
  4. Waiting for the perfect idea (never starting). Target the middle: a good starting point with enough interesting qualities to morph in the right direction.

- **Ten questions to evaluate an idea**
  1. **Founder–market fit** (the most important): is this team obviously right for it? (PlanGrid: construction-industry veteran + strong iPad developer.) Recast the search as "good idea *for my team*".
  2. **Market size**: ~$1B+, either big now or small but growing fast (Coinbase in 2012).
  3. **Acuteness**: best case, the alternative is literally nothing (Brex: banks would not issue startups a corporate card).
  4. **Competition**: usually a good sign; against entrenched players you need a new insight.
  5. Do you want it personally, or know people who do? If neither, go talk to users.
  6. Did it **recently become possible or necessary** (new tech, regulation, new problem)? Checkr: gig platforms needed mass background checks; incumbents didn't fit the new use case.
  7. **Proxy**: a large non-competing company doing something similar validates it (DoorDash as proxy for Rappi in Latin America).
  8. Would you work on it for years? Passion often grows as the idea starts working; don't over-weight initial excitement.
  9. **Scalable?** Pure software yes. Trouble spot: services (agencies, dev shops) needing skilled human labour per customer.
  10. **Good idea space?** (Dalton's concept: one abstraction up, e.g. "software for hospitals".) Hit rates differ by orders of magnitude across spaces; past decade fintech infrastructure and enterprise vertical SaaS were high, consumer hardware, social networks, ad tech low (spaces flip over time). A fertile space plus founder fit means adjacent ideas are available if the first one fails. Fivetran kept pitching different data tools to the same companies and stumbled into the real need.

- **Three things that make ideas look bad but are good** (others avoid them, leaving them on the table)
  - **Hard to start / schlep blindness** (PG): Stripe required bank deals and card-infrastructure knowledge, which scared off thousands of devs who felt the pain.
  - **Boring spaces**: Gusto/payroll. Boring ideas have higher hit rates than fun ones (restaurant/music discovery). Day-to-day work (code, bugs, users) is the same either way, so "fun-sounding" doesn't predict enjoyment 6–12 months in.
  - **Existing competitors**: no competitors often means no demand. Dropbox was ~20th in cloud storage, but most people used none of them, proving an unsolved problem; Drew's insight was OS-level automatic sync instead of manual web upload.

- **Generating ideas**
  - ≥70% of top-100 YC companies got their idea organically, not by brainstorming; brainstorming tends to produce tar pits.
  - Long game: become an expert in something valuable (e.g. work at a startup); build things you find interesting (Replit began as a side interest).
  - Seven recipes, best first:
    1. Start from what your team is especially good at (automatic founder–market fit). Rezi founders (real estate + debt finance) only searched that intersection. Odd how often experts apply with unrelated ideas.
    2. A problem you personally hit, ideally from an unusual vantage point (Vetcove: founders' vet father ordered supplies by phone; vets rarely start startups, so no competition). Exercise: for each founder, list every job, internship, and life experience; ask what problems you saw and what you know that others don't.
    3. Things you personally wish existed (DoorDash). Most likely to lead to tar pits: ask why it doesn't exist yet.
    4. Recent changes in the world (COVID → Gather Town).
    5. New variants of recently successful companies (Nuvocargo = Flexport for Latin America, chosen analytically: big market, proxies, founder connections, fit with ops-heavy work, despite no domain expertise).
    6. Talk to people about their problems, but first pick a fertile space, and also talk to founders in that space. AtoB: young founders picked trucking software (big, under-digitised), drove to truck stops to interview drivers, talked to trucking founders, built a mental map, cycled through many ideas, landed on fuel cards. Most won't do it because it sounds like too much work.
    7. Big industries that seem broken. Bonus: join a co-founder who already has an idea.
  - Final rule: you often can't tell if an idea is good; the only sure test is to launch.

- **Relevance to Sam**
  - Recipe 1–2 point straight at Sam's unusual vantage: large-scale travel location data, supplier API integrations (Viator, RateHawk), GTFS transit data, Nigerian personal finance pain, Nigerian B2B commodity export. Ideas at these intersections have built-in founder–market fit, and few Lagos-based builders see them.
  - Tereka: check proxies (successful budgeting apps elsewhere that haven't caught on in Nigeria) and "recently became necessary" (inflation/naira volatility). Watch the tar-pit risk: consumer budgeting is a classic graveyard (note: Plaid started as a failed budgeting app; see Todd Jackson notes). Find the hard part others hit (retention, monetisation of free users) before building more.
  - Riselist: Q9 flags agencies as hard to scale. That's fine as a service-first wedge, but plan where software or productisation removes skilled labour per client.
  - Data pipelines/supplier integrations are classic schlep: unglamorous, hard to start, and therefore under-competed.

## Aaron Epstein (YC) — Startup Business Models and Pricing | Startup School (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oWZbWzAyHAE)
**Copy a proven business model (recurring, close to the transaction, scales with software) and innovate on product; then charge early, price on value, and expect that you are undercharging.**

- **Nine business models behind nearly every $1B company**: SaaS, transactional (take a cut of flows, often fintech), marketplace, hard tech, usage-based, enterprise, advertising, e-commerce, bio. Founders frustrated that investors won't fund them and growth stalls are often using an unproven model. Early-stage: pick ONE model.
- **YC top-100 analysis**
  - SaaS 31%, transactional 22%, marketplaces 14%: these three = 67%. Advertising and e-commerce barely appear.
  - Power law: top 10 = 50% of total value. 5 of the top 10 are marketplaces (Airbnb, Instacart, DoorDash, OpenSea, Faire): 14% of companies but ~30% of value. Marketplaces are hard to start (chicken-and-egg: solve supply and demand together) but after the inflection get network effects and tend to winner-take-all.
  - 3 of top 10 transactional (Stripe, Coinbase, Brex): 22% of companies, 29% of value. Reason: in the flow of funds, easy to take a cut, become critical infrastructure solving a top-3 problem, painful to rip out. "Get as close to the transaction as possible."
  - Advertising: only 3%. Needs organic virality and becoming the hub (Reddit, Twitch). Rule: don't use ads as the primary model unless you expect to be a top-10 site.
  - Absent from the list: services/consulting (non-recurring, scales with people, low margin; fine as a way to *learn* what to build); affiliate (too far from the transaction: acquire user, send elsewhere, hope they buy, get a small commission 30–90 days later); hardware (capital-intensive, low margin); businesses built on another platform (platform risk: if you work, the platform can shut you down and capture the revenue).
- **Recurring revenue and retention math**
  - Recurring revenue: predictable, compounds, higher LTV, lower effective CAC (no re-acquiring). Only works with strong retention: the product must keep delivering value.
  - 95% monthly retention → 100 customers become 54 after 12 months (0.95^12 ≈ 0.54); need 46 new just to stand still. 90% monthly → 28 left (0.9^12 ≈ 0.28). A 5-point difference in monthly retention can be life or death. You can't scale a leaky bucket.
- **Moats**: network effects (marketplaces); lock-in/switching costs (Stripe; customer data that disappears if they leave); enterprise (long sales cycles but low churn after); deep technical innovation (Cruise, Boom: years to catch up); economies of scale/margins (DoorDash, Instacart); organic distribution (virality/word of mouth beats competitors who pay for users).
- **Recap of best-business traits**: recurring revenue, high retention, defensible moat, close to the transaction, scales with software not people, a business model customers already understand. New product, familiar model.

- **Pricing (five insights)**
  1. **Charge.** The most common mistake. Fear of "no", of churn, of losing to competitors. Charging tells you: whether users will pay at all (often binary), which segment is most willing (segment A vs B), how much, and if nobody pays, that value is too low or the segment is wrong. Pricing also tells you which acquisition channels you can afford. Stripe priced ~5% vs competitors' ~3% to test whether quick signup and great docs carried real value, rather than undercutting.
     - Don't overthink: find the right **order of magnitude**. $10 vs willingness of $100 → fix. $10 vs $15–20 → fine for now. It takes years to capture full value.
  2. **Price on value, not cost.** Cost → price = your margin; price → perceived value = room to raise. Cost-plus ignores value. Cost > price = negative margins (unscalable); price > value = no sale.
     - Find value: ask customers (and signed-up non-payers) "what problem were you hoping we'd solve?" Answers cluster into: make more money, reduce costs, move faster, reduce risk/compliance headache.
     - Or raise prices incrementally until pushback. Ideal price = **customers complain but still pay** (they "think about it" and come back a week later). Instant enthusiastic yes = too cheap.
  3. **You are almost certainly undercharging.** "Like competitor X but cheaper" is not a sustainable advantage: a bigger rival can price below your cost until you die. Higher prices → higher margins → can outspend rivals on acquisition. Price signals value (low price can read as low quality). Raising prices is the easiest revenue lever: doubling price on 1,000 customers doubles revenue without acquiring 1,000 more.
     - If users won't pay more: build more value, or move to a bigger (top-3) problem. Legit reasons to discount: very first user (feedback), recognisable logo (social proof), lock-in (data on platform), and confident renewal at a higher price.
  4. **Pricing isn't permanent.** Grandfather existing customers and raise for new ones, or give advance notice and add value; sticky products see little churn. Netflix raised repeatedly and has 221M subscribers. If you fear these are the only customers you'll ever get, the business is wrong.
  5. **Keep it simple.** Quicken page: five buy buttons, crossed-out prices, confusing → lower conversion. GitLab: three clear plans.
  - Segment story: free → sheepishly $10/month ($120/yr); customers replied they hoped it cost more or they'd worry about trusting it with their data. Sales advisor demanded quoting $120K/yr; buyer countered $12K; settled at $18K (150x). Kept pushing to six figures; acquired by Twilio for >$3B.

- **Relevance to Sam**
  - Tereka is free, consumer, finance: the advertising model is a poor primary path (top-10-site rule). Better: transactional (in the flow of funds: savings, bill payment, "cup" transfers) or a paid tier/B2B (employers, cooperatives, banks) model. Avoid relying on another platform's goodwill (platform risk) for core function.
  - Riselist (services) is not venture-scale as-is but is a valid learning wedge; productise what you learn. Price on brand outcomes (sales, reach), not hours.
  - Trameter / Drive Button: affiliate commissions are "far from the transaction" (Viator-style commissions paid weeks later). The memory note about an in-house booking aim fits the advice: owning the booking moves Trameter into the flow of funds.
  - In Nigeria, users may still read very low prices as low trust for money products (the Segment lesson). Test prices by order of magnitude in naira, not by copying US pricing.

## Tom Blomfield (YC partner, "Tom") — How To Price For B2B | Startup School (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4hjiRmgmHiU)
**B2B price = about one third of the value you provably create (the "value equation"), floored by cost and defended by differentiation rather than price wars.**

- **The problem**: founders who have never worked at a big company anchor on consumer SaaS ($19–49/month) and freeze when a champion asks for a price, because asking for tens or hundreds of thousands feels absurd.

- **Element 1: the value equation (gets you 80–90% of the way)**
  - Sit with the champion (the person at the customer who wants your product) and write down, step by step, what your product will do for their company: save time (= cost), cut cost directly, or increase revenue. Have them challenge and stress-test the assumptions, because it is the document they will take to their boss/CFO.
  - Worked example: 100 support agents × $50K salary + $50K overhead = $100K fully loaded each = $10M/year. AI tool removes 20% of queries/time → $2M value. Price at **25–50% of value** (rule: they keep ~2/3, you keep ~1/3) → ~$700K/year.
  - The equation also defines **pilot success criteria**: e.g. trial with 10 agents for a month; if it saves ≥20% the equation holds. If it saves 15% or 25%, adjust price accordingly.

- **Element 2: cost is a floor, never the starting point**
  - Cost-plus pricing always underprices software. Check: $700K price vs $200K cost (LLM API + cloud) = fine. If your 1/3 share is $150K and cost is $200K, it's a bad business: show more value, change what you build, or exit.
  - Target 80–90% gross margin. Treat cloud/AI credits (AWS, Microsoft, OpenAI) as cash costs; they run out and will wreck margins.
  - Pricing at/below cost is a risky land-grab bet on future cost declines. LLM inference prices do keep dropping, which gives slight room to price lower now, but he still advises holding 80–90% margin.

- **Element 3: competition**
  - If an equivalent competitor undercuts by 50%, don't start a price war (race to the bottom; commodities lose all margin; airlines average ~2.7% net margin). Differentiate on functionality/value: pick a niche, specific integrations or industries, so it's not apples to apples.

- **Structure and tactics**
  - Ask the champion how they pay for similar software (flat monthly, per seat, usage bands, credits) and mirror it. Buyers distrust uncapped usage pricing; add a cap.
  - Keep it simple; complex pricing kills sales processes.
  - Prefer committed MRR/ARR over pure usage: contracted revenue survives a downturn until renewal; usage revenue can fall off a cliff in a bad month (investors worry). Technique: start new customers on usage for 1–2 months, then offer a flat commitment slightly below observed usage (using ~$15K/month → $12K/month flat for a 12-month commit).
  - Ask the champion's personal **signing authority** (e.g. up to $15K without CFO/legal) and price the pilot just under it to move fast. (note: transcript says "$149.99"; context implies ~$14,999.)
  - **Published price vs "contact sales"**: value differs per enterprise, so a single public enterprise price overprices for low-value buyers (lost) and underprices for high-value ones. Common pattern: one or two cheap self-serve plans (individual, small team) + enterprise plan gated on features enterprises need and small users don't (SOC 2 reports, SSO, audit logs, compliance reports, data residency). Enterprise can be up to ~10x more.
  - **Price dictates sales channel.** Rule of thumb: a salesperson should close ~5x their total compensation in new ARR ($100K comp → $500K new ARR/year). That could be one $500K whale, 20 × $25K deals (~2/month: doable for an AE), or 500 × $1K deals (~42/month, ~2 per working day: that's an inside-sales call centre, not field sales).
  - **Trials/pilots**: long free trials/pilots are counterproductive (customer isn't committed). Keep proofs of concept to 2–4 weeks with value-equation success criteria. Better if confident: an annual contract from day one with a 30–60-day no-questions money-back opt-out; it counts as recurring revenue immediately.
  - Don't fake size (padding the team page or LinkedIn). Play startup strengths: founders' phone numbers, on call 24/7, which Salesforce/Oracle can't offer.
  - **If you truly can't price**: start near what customers pay for similar software, then raise ~50% with each new pitch ($10K → $15K → ~$22K…) until you lose >25% of deals on price alone. Winning every deal instantly means you're underpriced.
  - Early 5–10 customers are a tiny fraction of 5-year revenue; over-optimising early pricing is a mistake. The first 2–3 sales are the hardest ever; just close them. Prices rise later with better product, new modules/paywalls, upsells, logos, and sales skill.

- **Relevance to Sam**
  - Riselist: build a value equation with each brand's champion (e.g. expected sales or cost-per-acquisition vs current ads spend), price around a third of the expected value, and define success metrics for a short paid pilot instead of free trials. Ask what they currently pay agencies or influencers and mirror that structure (per campaign/retainer).
  - Trameter B2B tools (e.g. Drive Button for bloggers/publishers, supplier data products): value equation in booking revenue uplift or engineering time saved. Gate enterprise features (SLAs, data residency, audit logs) for larger partners.
  - Any LLM-powered feature Sam builds: count API costs and free credits as real cash; target 80–90% gross margin.
  - Small/solo team: lean into founder access (WhatsApp line to the founder) as a selling point instead of pretending to be big.

## Dalton Caldwell & Michael Seibel — How To Talk To Customers (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zuy4b0DVjS0)
**Talk to customers to collect surprising clues, not orders: the customer is the expert on the problem, you must be the expert on the solution, and the valuable insight is the one you could not have predicted.**

- **Mental models / first principles**
  - By default founders avoid customer conversations and substitute proxies: surveys, "Steve Jobs just imagined it", copying competitors or what OpenAI/Google launched, retweets. Dalton guesses ~70% of pre-PMF YC companies are largely copying competitors' features ("X has it, so we need it").
  - The hidden harmful second half of "talk to your customers" is "...and build the features they ask for." First half true, second half false. **Waiter anti-pattern**: going to the table, writing down the order, building it.
  - Why it fails: to reach billions of users (consumer) or billions in revenue (B2B) you need clues gathered from customers and creatively synthesised in your own head. "Faster horse."
  - **Customer = expert on the problem; founder = expert on the solution.** Taking orders is lazy; if it worked, everyone would build Google.
  - **Efficient-markets check**: if a problem is old and there is economic incentive to fix it (factories), assume the obvious solutions have been tried; the real solution is probably counterintuitive. Saying "healthcare/insurance is broken" is not an insight.
  - Seibel's question (attributed to PB, Paul Buchheit): **where is the value being created?** Transcribing feature requests creates little; synthesising a truly new approach is where you get paid.
  - **Humans are next-token predictors**: if you can predict exactly what users will say, you won't find anything new. If 100 interviews all say the same thing, you're probably asking the wrong questions. Be a detective for shocking, weird, counterintuitive tokens, things competitors wouldn't copy because they wouldn't understand why you did them. (Note: this contrasts with Todd Jackson's "stop when you can predict 70–80%" rule. Reconcile: predictability means you've mapped the problem space; stop *validating* there, but keep *exploring* for anomalies.)

- **Mechanisms / how to do it**
  - **Be in their workspace; watch them work.** Users normalise pain (clicking 17 times to update a form is "just my job") and won't think to complain. You only see it by watching.
  - Anti-pattern: building for factories without knowing any factories or having visited one. You must like and want to spend time with the users. Not knowing the domain is fine; incuriosity is not. Learning takes more than one one-hour visit.
  - **B2B: understand how the customer's business makes money and how your user contributes.** Saving an HR person three clicks may delight them but create little value; helping them make an offer faster so the company lands an engineer is the real value. Employees are often unclear on how they create value, so talk to their boss and colleagues too.
  - **Use AI for table-stakes domain knowledge**: you can reach ~50th-percentile understanding of, e.g., running a factory by talking to a model and reading what's public about the company; ask "if I were this user trying to make this company better, what would I do?" That makes each real visit far more valuable and helps filter user problems.

- **Examples of counterintuitive insights (obvious only in hindsight)**
  - Google: search looked "done"; faster results → people search more.
  - DoorDash: delivery looked solved (delivery.com), but suburbs couldn't get non-pizza/Chinese delivery. Invisible to young city-dwelling techies who assumed suburban families cook every night.
  - Gmail's 1GB storage: incumbents thought email was finished; PB watched people waste time on filing systems and deleting mail.
  - ChatGPT: OpenAI was API-only for years; a simple chat harness became the killer app. A mocked "dirty hack" of ChatGPT writing code turned out to be the future.

- **Rules of thumb**
  - Never treat surveys, competitor feature lists, or launch announcements as substitutes for conversations and observation.
  - Ask problem and workflow questions, not "what features do you want?"
  - Expect to do something different and weird; "saves some clicks" rarely wins.
  - Always be able to point to where your startup creates value.

- **Relevance to Sam**
  - Tereka: sit with Nigerians while they actually manage money (bank apps, ajo/esusu groups, WhatsApp notes, cash envelopes). Look for normalised pain and weird workarounds instead of asking what features they want. "Organising money" is broken but not an insight; the counterintuitive behavioural clue is the product.
  - Riselist: understand how each brand makes money (which SKUs, which margins, which sales channel), not just "they want views". Value = sales or customer acquisition, not post count.
  - Trameter: watch travel bloggers and travellers book. Where does the Drive Button create value for the blogger's business (revenue per article) vs. just saving clicks?
  - As a solo builder with limited time, use AI to get baseline domain knowledge before every interview so the scarce real conversations go deep.
